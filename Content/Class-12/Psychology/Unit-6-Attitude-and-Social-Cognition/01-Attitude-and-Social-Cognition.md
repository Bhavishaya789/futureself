# Attitude and Social Cognition
minutes: 40
yt: class 12 psychology attitude social cognition cognitive dissonance prejudice stereotype attribution schema helping behaviour
@study
An attitude is a state of mind with evaluative feelings about a person, object or idea. It has three components: affective (feelings), behavioural (action tendency) and cognitive (beliefs), the ABC components. Attitudes are formed by learning: association, reinforcement and modelling, through family and school, reference groups, personal experience and the media. Attitude change depends on source, message and target. Festinger’s cognitive dissonance theory (1957) says inconsistency among beliefs or between attitude and behaviour creates discomfort that motivates change; Heider’s balance theory describes harmony among a person, other and object. Persuasion is stronger when the source is credible and attractive. Attitudes can be measured by Likert’s scale (1932), Thurstone’s scale, the semantic differential and Bogardus’s social distance scale. A stereotype is a generalised belief about a group, prejudice is a preconceived negative attitude towards a group, and discrimination is unfair behaviour. Prejudice is reduced by intergroup contact (Allport’s contact hypothesis), superordinate goals (Sherif) and education, and by challenging stereotypes. Social cognition is how we process information about the social world. Schemas are mental frameworks that organise knowledge. Impression formation: Asch (1946) showed that central traits like warm and cold shape impressions. Attribution is explaining behaviour: internal (dispositional) or external (situational); Kelley’s covariation model uses consensus, distinctiveness and consistency. Biases include the fundamental attribution error (overestimating dispositional causes), the actor-observer effect and the self-serving bias. Presence of others affects behaviour: social facilitation (Triplett, 1898; Zajonc) improves performance on simple tasks, and social loafing (Ringelmann) reduces effort in groups. Pro-social behaviour includes helping and altruism; the bystander effect (Latane and Darley) shows that more onlookers reduce helping because of diffusion of responsibility. Aggression has been explained by the frustration-aggression hypothesis and social learning.
## Deep dive: Attitude
ABC components; formed by learning; changed by source, message and target; dissonance motivates change.
## Deep dive: Prejudice
Stereotype, prejudice and discrimination; contact and superordinate goals reduce them.
## Deep dive: Social cognition
Schemas, attribution and biases explain how we judge others; bystander effect and social loafing in groups.
