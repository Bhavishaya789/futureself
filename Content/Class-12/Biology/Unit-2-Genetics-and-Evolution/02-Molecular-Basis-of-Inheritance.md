# Molecular Basis of Inheritance

@study
DNA is a long polymer of deoxyribonucleotides; each nucleotide has a nitrogenous base, a pentose sugar (deoxyribose) and a phosphate group. Purines are adenine and guanine; pyrimidines are cytosine, thymine (in DNA) and uracil (in RNA). Chargaff's rule: A = T and G = C so that purines equal pyrimidines. Watson and Crick (1953) proposed the double-helix model, based on X-ray diffraction data from Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins: two antiparallel polynucleotide chains held by hydrogen bonds (A-T two bonds; G-C three bonds); the helix has a pitch of 3.4 nm with 10 base pairs per turn and the distance between base pairs is 0.34 nm. Nucleosome: DNA wrapped around a histone octamer (H2A, H2B, H3, H4, each twice), about 200 bp; histones are positively charged basic proteins; euchromatin is loosely packed and active, heterochromatin is densely packed and inactive. Griffith (1928) discovered transformation in Streptococcus pneumoniae; Avery, MacLeod and McCarty showed that DNA is the transforming principle; Hershey and Chase (1952) used radioactive 32P and 35S in bacteriophages to prove that DNA is the genetic material. Meselson and Stahl (1958) used 15N and E. coli to prove semiconservative replication of DNA. Replication begins at the origin of replication; helicase unwinds the helix; DNA polymerase adds nucleotides only in the 5' to 3' direction; the leading strand is continuous and the lagging strand is discontinuous (Okazaki fragments) joined by DNA ligase. RNA types: mRNA, tRNA (adapter, cloverleaf) and rRNA (structural and catalytic). Transcription: DNA-dependent RNA polymerase synthesises RNA from the template strand (3' to 5') in the 5' to 3' direction using a promoter and terminator; in bacteria one RNA polymerase; in eukaryotes RNA polymerase I (rRNA), II (mRNA) and III (tRNA, 5S rRNA). In eukaryotes, hnRNA undergoes splicing (introns removed, exons joined), capping (methyl guanosine triphosphate at the 5' end) and tailing (polyadenylation at the 3' end). Genetic code: triplet; 64 codons; 61 code for amino acids; AUG is the start codon (methionine); UAA, UAG and UGA are stop codons; the code is degenerate, unambiguous, universal and non-overlapping; Nirenberg, Khorana and Matthaei deciphered it. Translation: ribosome (30S and 50S in prokaryotes forming 70S) uses mRNA to make a polypeptide; aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase charges tRNA; peptide bonds form between amino acids. Regulation of gene expression: Jacob and Monod proposed the lac operon in E. coli (1961): structural genes lacZ, lacY, lacA; promoter, operator and the regulatory gene lacI that codes for a repressor; lactose (inducer) binds the repressor, allowing transcription — it is an inducible system under negative regulation. Human Genome Project (1990 to 2003): about 3 billion base pairs; around 30,000 genes (later estimates about 20,000 to 25,000); chromosome 1 has the most genes (2968) and Y the fewest (231); only about 1.5 percent of the genome codes for proteins; over 99.9 percent of bases are the same in all humans; SNPs (single nucleotide polymorphisms) number about 1.4 million; Methods: ESTs and sequence annotation. DNA fingerprinting (Alec Jeffreys) uses VNTRs (variable number of tandem repeats) and Southern blotting; uses include forensics and paternity tests.
## Deep dive: Structure of DNA
Two antiparallel chains with A-T and G-C pairing form a double helix. Histones package DNA into nucleosomes. Experiments proved DNA is the genetic material.
## Deep dive: Central dogma
DNA replicates semiconservatively, is transcribed to RNA and translated into protein. The genetic code is triplet and degenerate.
## Deep dive: Regulation and genome
The lac operon shows negative regulation. The Human Genome Project mapped about 3 billion base pairs. DNA fingerprinting uses VNTRs.
