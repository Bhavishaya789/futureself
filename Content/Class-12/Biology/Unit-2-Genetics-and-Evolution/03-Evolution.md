@study
Origin of life: the universe is about 20 billion years old and Earth about 4.5 billion years. Big Bang theory. Oparin and Haldane proposed that the first form of life could have come from pre-existing non-living organic molecules (chemical evolution). Stanley Miller (1953) simulated early earth conditions (CH4, H2, NH3 and water vapour at 800 degrees Celsius) in a closed flask and formed amino acids, supporting chemical evolution. The first non-cellular forms may have been RNA, and the first cellular forms anaerobic heterotrophs. Louis Pasteur disproved spontaneous generation. Evidences of evolution: fossils (study is palaeontology; the age of fossils is known by radioactive dating), comparative anatomy and morphology: homologous organs (similar structure, different function; forelimbs of whale, bat, cheetah and human; divergent evolution) and analogous organs (different structure, same function; wings of butterfly and bird; convergent evolution); vestigial organs; biochemical similarities; adaptive radiation: Darwin's finches in the Galapagos islands, and Australian marsupials; convergent evolution: Australian marsupials and placental mammals. Adaptive radiation is evolution of different species in a geographical area starting from a point. Lamarck proposed the theory of use and disuse of organs and inheritance of acquired characters (giraffe neck). Darwin's theory (On the Origin of Species, 1859, after the voyage of HMS Beagle): branching descent and natural selection; the observations are that populations produce more offspring than survive, variations exist, and the fittest survive (differential reproduction). Alfred Wallace independently reached similar conclusions based on the Malay Archipelago. Industrial melanism: in England, the proportion of dark-coloured peppered moths (Biston betularia) rose after industrialisation because soot-covered tree trunks favoured dark moths. Mutation theory: Hugo de Vries proposed that mutations (large, discontinuous variations) cause speciation (saltation). Hardy-Weinberg principle: allelic frequencies in a population are stable and constant from generation to generation; p + q = 1; p2 + 2pq + q2 = 1; the gene pool remains constant (genetic equilibrium). Factors that disturb equilibrium: gene migration or gene flow, genetic drift, mutation, genetic recombination and natural selection. Genetic drift is a change in allele frequency by chance; founder effect is when drift occurs in a small group of colonisers. Natural selection can be stabilising, directional or disruptive. Speciation involves reproductive isolation. Origin of man: Dryopithecus and Ramapithecus were hairy and walked like gorillas and chimpanzees; Australopithecus (about 2 mya) ate fruit; Homo habilis (about 2 mya; brain capacity 650 to 800 cc; probably did not eat meat); Homo erectus (about 1.5 mya; brain 900 cc; ate meat); Neanderthal man (brain 1400 cc; lived 100,000 to 40,000 years ago; used hides to protect their body and buried their dead); Homo sapiens arose in Africa about 75,000 to 10,000 years ago; modern Homo sapiens arose in the Middle East about 34,000 years ago. Cave art developed about 18,000 years ago; agriculture about 10,000 years ago.
## Deep dive: Origin of life
Chemical evolution preceded biological evolution. The Miller experiment formed amino acids from simple gases. First cells were anaerobic heterotrophs.
## Deep dive: Evidence and mechanism
Fossils, homology, analogy and biogeography support evolution. Natural selection acts on variation. Industrial melanism is a field example.
## Deep dive: Population genetics and humans
Hardy-Weinberg describes equilibrium; drift, flow, mutation and selection disturb it. Human evolution proceeded from Australopithecus to Homo sapiens.
