# Biodiversity and Conservation
minutes: 45
yt: class 12 biology biodiversity conservation hotspots extinction patterns in situ ex situ
@study
Biodiversity is the variety of life on Earth; Edward Wilson popularised the term. Levels: genetic diversity (a species with variation in genes; India has more than 50,000 genetically different strains of rice and 1,000 varieties of mango), species diversity (Western Ghats have more amphibian species than the Eastern Ghats) and ecological diversity (India has deserts, rain forests, mangroves, coral reefs, wetlands, estuaries and alpine meadows, hence a greater ecosystem diversity than Norway). Globally about 1.5 million species have been described; Robert May estimated 7 million; more than 70 percent of the species recorded are animals, of which insects are more than 70 percent; plants (including algae, fungi, bryophytes, gymnosperms and angiosperms) constitute no more than 22 percent. India has 2.4 percent of the world's land area but 8.1 percent of global species diversity; it is one of the 12 mega-diversity countries. Patterns of biodiversity: latitudinal gradient (species diversity decreases from equator to poles; Colombia has 1,400 bird species, New York 105 and Greenland 56; India has 1,200). Reasons: the tropics have been undisturbed for millions of years (more time for species diversification), stable and predictable climate promotes niche specialisation, and more solar energy produces higher productivity. Species-area relationship (Alexander von Humboldt): within a region, species richness increases with increasing explored area but only up to a limit; log S = log C + Z log A, where S is species richness, A is area, Z is the slope of the line (regression coefficient), and C is the Y-intercept; the value of Z lies between 0.1 and 0.2 irrespective of the taxonomic group or region; for very large areas such as continents, the slope is steeper (0.6 to 1.2). Importance: the loss of biodiversity leads to decline in plant production, lowered resistance to environmental perturbations and increased variability in ecosystem processes. Stability: David Tilman showed that plots with more species showed less year-to-year variation in total biomass. Paul Ehrlich's rivet popper hypothesis. Loss of biodiversity: 784 extinctions (359 invertebrates, 338 vertebrates and 87 plants) in the last 500 years (the Dodo, quagga, thylacine, Steller's sea cow, three subspecies of tiger: Bali, Javan and Caspian). The Amazon rainforest is called the lungs of the planet. The Sixth Extinction is in progress. Causes of loss, the evil quartet: habitat loss and fragmentation (the most important cause), over-exploitation, alien species invasions (Nile perch in Lake Victoria; water hyacinth; Parthenium; African catfish Clarias gariepinus) and co-extinctions. Conservation: in situ (on site): national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, biosphere reserves, sacred groves and biodiversity hotspots. Hotspots are regions with very high levels of species richness and high endemism; there are 34 hotspots in the world; three in India: Western Ghats and Sri Lanka, Indo-Burma and the Himalaya. Ex situ (off site): zoological parks, botanical gardens, wildlife safari parks, cryopreservation (gene banks) and seed banks. The Convention on Biological Diversity (Rio de Janeiro Earth Summit 1992), the World Summit on Sustainable Development (Johannesburg, 2002) and the Biological Diversity Act (India, 2002).
## Deep dive: Levels and patterns
Genetic, species and ecological diversity make up biodiversity. Diversity is highest in the tropics and rises with area.
## Deep dive: Loss
Habitat loss, over-exploitation, alien species and co-extinctions drive extinction.
## Deep dive: Conservation
In situ methods protect habitats; ex situ methods protect species outside. Hotspots set conservation priorities.
