# Organisms and Populations

@study
Ecology is the study of interactions among organisms and between the organism and its physical (abiotic) environment. Levels of biological organisation: organisms, populations, communities, ecosystems, biomes and the biosphere. Major abiotic factors are temperature, water, light and soil. Responses to abiotic factors: regulate (thermoregulation and osmoregulation: birds and mammals), conform (most animals and plants), migrate (Siberian cranes to Keoladeo National Park, Bharatpur) and suspend (hibernation, aestivation, diapause, spores and seeds). Adaptations: kangaroo rat in North American deserts meets its water needs by internal fat oxidation and concentrates its urine; desert plants have a thick cuticle and sunken stomata, and CAM photosynthesis; Opuntia has no leaves and spines instead; mammals from colder climates have shorter ears and limbs to minimise heat loss (Allen's rule); in the polar seas aquatic mammals have a thick layer of fat (blubber). Many fish thrive in Antarctic waters at sub-zero temperature. Organisms in high altitudes face altitude sickness (low atmospheric pressure, body response includes increased red blood cell production, decreased binding capacity of haemoglobin and increased breathing rate). Water: stenothermal organisms tolerate a narrow range of temperature; eurythermal tolerate a wide range; stenohaline tolerate a narrow range of salinity; euryhaline a wide one. Behaviour: desert lizards bask in the sun and move into shade. Population attributes: birth rate (natality), death rate (mortality), sex ratio, age distribution (pre-reproductive, reproductive and post-reproductive; age pyramids may be expanding, stable or declining) and population density. Population size is measured by the number, biomass or percent cover; Pt+1 = Pt + [(N + I) - (M + E)], where N is natality, M is mortality, I is immigration and E is emigration. Growth models: exponential (J-shaped) growth: dN/dt = rN; N(t) = N0 e^rt; where r is the intrinsic rate of natural increase. Logistic (S-shaped) growth with limited resources: dN/dt = rN (K - N)/K; K is the carrying capacity. Life history variation: organisms evolve to maximise their reproductive fitness (r-selected and K-selected). Population interactions: mutualism (+ +; lichens, mycorrhiza, fig and wasp, orchid Ophrys and bee through sexual deceit), competition (- -; Gause's competitive exclusion principle: two closely related species competing for the same resources cannot coexist indefinitely; resource partitioning enables coexistence, as with MacArthur's warblers), predation (+ -; keeps prey populations in check; cactus Opuntia controlled by the cactus-feeding predator Cactoblastis in Australia), parasitism (+ -; ectoparasites and endoparasites, brood parasitism of koel and crow), commensalism (+ 0; epiphytes on mango, barnacles on whales, cattle egret and cattle) and amensalism (- 0; Penicillium and bacteria). Prey defences: camouflage, toxic chemicals (Monarch butterfly), thorns and spines. Plant defences: nicotine, caffeine, quinine, strychnine and opium; Calotropis produces poisonous cardiac glycosides.
## Deep dive: Responses and adaptations
Organisms regulate, conform, migrate or suspend. Adaptations suit deserts, cold and altitude.
## Deep dive: Population attributes
Birth, death, immigration and emigration change population size. Age pyramids show structure.
## Deep dive: Growth and interactions
Exponential and logistic growth models describe growth. Species interactions include mutualism, competition, predation and parasitism.

P: Explain Gause’s competitive exclusion principle.
A: Two closely related species competing for the same limited resources cannot coexist indefinitely; the superior competitor eliminates the other.
