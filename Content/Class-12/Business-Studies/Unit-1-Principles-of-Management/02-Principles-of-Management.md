# Principles of Management

@study
Principles of management are broad and general guidelines for decision making and behaviour. Features: universal applicability, general guidelines, formed by practice and experimentation, flexible, mainly behavioural, cause-effect relationship and contingent. Significance: providing managers with useful insights into reality, optimum utilisation of resources and effective administration, scientific decisions, meeting changing environment requirements, fulfilling social responsibility and management training and research. Henri Fayol (1841 to 1925) was a French mining engineer; his book is General and Industrial Management (1916); he identified fourteen principles: (1) Division of work (specialisation), (2) Authority and responsibility (authority is the right to give orders; responsibility the obligation; they go together), (3) Discipline (obedience to rules), (4) Unity of command (one boss for one subordinate), (5) Unity of direction (one head and one plan for a group of activities with the same objective), (6) Subordination of individual interest to general interest, (7) Remuneration of employees (fair), (8) Centralisation and decentralisation, (9) Scalar chain (formal lines of authority from highest to lowest; Gang plank is the shortcut or direct contact in emergencies), (10) Order (right place for people and things), (11) Equity (fairness; kindness and justice), (12) Stability of personnel, (13) Initiative and (14) Esprit de corps (union is strength; teamwork). Fayol's principles are flexible; his five functions are forecasting, planning, organising, commanding and controlling, and he divided business activities into technical, commercial, financial, security, accounting and managerial. F.W. Taylor (1856 to 1915), the father of scientific management, wrote The Principles of Scientific Management (1911). His principles: (1) Science, not rule of thumb, (2) Harmony, not discord, (3) Cooperation, not individualism, (4) Development of each and every person to his or her greatest efficiency and prosperity. Techniques of scientific management: (a) Functional foremanship: eight specialists, four in the planning department (route clerk, instruction card clerk, time and cost clerk, disciplinarian) and four in the production department (speed boss, gang boss, repair boss, inspector); (b) Standardisation and simplification of work (standardisation of processes, raw materials, product, machines and working conditions; simplification eliminates unnecessary diversity); (c) Method study (one best way); (d) Motion study (movements of workers; unnecessary movements eliminated); (e) Time study (standard time for each task) and (f) Fatigue study (rest intervals); (g) Differential piece wage system (higher rate for efficient workers; for example, Rs 10 per piece for 100 pieces or more and Rs 5 for fewer). Taylor's mental revolution means a change in attitude of workers and management towards each other: both should focus on increasing the surplus. Taylor and Fayol compared: Fayol gave general management at top level and is a practitioner; Taylor focused on shop floor and was a scientist; Fayol's principles apply universally, Taylor's are specific. Both complement each other.
## Deep dive: Principles
Management principles are universal, flexible guidelines. They help in decisions, resource use and social responsibility.
## Deep dive: Fayol
Fayol gave fourteen principles for general management such as unity of command, scalar chain and esprit de corps.
## Deep dive: Taylor
Taylor proposed scientific management based on science, harmony, cooperation and development. His techniques include time and motion study.
