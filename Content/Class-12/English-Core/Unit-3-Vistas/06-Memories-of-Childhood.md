# Memories of Childhood
minutes: 45

@study
Memories of Childhood has two autobiographical accounts of discrimination. Part I: The Cutting of My Long Hair by Zitkala-Sa (Gertrude Simmons Bonnin), a Native American (Sioux) woman, describes her experience at the white missionary boarding school (Carlisle Indian School) in the United States, where she is forced to give up her tribal ways. She is excited about a trip to the East but finds the school harsh: she is woken by a bell, made to follow rules, and at the dining hall must bow and eat with knives and forks. When she learns that her long hair will be cut, she is horrified, for in her tribe only cowards and mourners had their hair cut and shingled; her mother had told her that only cowards had their hair shingled. She hides under a bed upstairs, but is found and dragged out, tied to a chair and her hair cut. She cries and feels that she has lost her individuality; she feels like a little animal driven by a herder. She is helpless against the cruelty and the loss of her tribe's culture. Part II: We Too Are Human Beings by Bama (Faustina Mary Fatima Rani), a Dalit writer from Tamil Nadu, describes how, as a child of the third class, she walks back from school and sees an elderly Dalit man carrying a packet of food by a string, without touching it, to a landlord of the upper caste. She finds this funny but her elder brother explains that it is not funny but a case of untouchability: the upper castes think Dalits are polluted. She is shocked and determined to excel; her brother advises her to study hard to get respect and wealth: if you study, you will have the respect denied to us; we must study with all our might, become the first in the class. She works hard and stands first in her class; many people become her friends. Themes: discrimination (racial and caste), loss of identity, the power of education to overcome oppression, resistance, dignity. Style: first person autobiographical narration, simple language, striking imagery.
## Deep dive: Zitkala-Sa
A Native American child is forcibly assimilated; the cutting of her hair is a symbol of lost identity.
## Deep dive: Bama
Untouchability shocks the child; her brother's advice to study hard becomes her route to dignity.
## Deep dive: Common ground
Both texts show discrimination and how a child learns to resist it; Bama turns to education while Zitkala-Sa mourns lost heritage.
