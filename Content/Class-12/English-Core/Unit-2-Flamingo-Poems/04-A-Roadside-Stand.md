# A Roadside Stand
minutes: 45
yt: class 12 english flamingo poem a roadside stand robert frost analysis
@study
A Roadside Stand is a poem by the American poet Robert Frost (1874-1963). It describes a small, poor roadside stand built by farmers on a country highway, a little pine-board stand that appeals to city motorists to stop and buy their produce. The poet empathises with these poor country people who have set up a stand to sell fruits, vegetables and other produce, hoping for a better life through the passing traffic. The poem begins with the stand put up with painted signs begging passersby to stop; but the cars passed by quickly, the drivers wanted to go faster and did not stop for the rural poor; few stopped, and the poet feels the hope of the sellers is ignored. The poet feels deeply for the rural poor who dream of getting money to meet their needs but are ignored; the farmers, he says, are tired of waiting for kindness. He is also critical of the city people, and of the government, whose plan for the rural poor is to create new towns and extend help but only in a slow way, leaving rural poverty unattended. The poet says that he wishes some kind of help could come, and how bitter it is that those who do the nation's hard work receive nothing; his heart aches that they have been cheated by the "greedy" city; the poet suspects that real help, which he calls selfless generosity, will not come. Themes: rural poverty versus urban indifference, the failure of social welfare, the false promise of progress, the cruelty of modern economic systems, empathy. Devices: irony, personification (the polished traffic), imagery, contrast (country vs city), rhyme scheme, tone of sympathy and anger. The poem reflects the Great Depression era in America, where farmers suffered.
## Deep dive: The stand
A pathetic little stand hoping for customers shows the poverty of the rural poor.
## Deep dive: The indifference
City motorists pass by and the government's promises do not reach rural people.
## Deep dive: The poet
Frost writes with sympathy and anger, hoping for true generosity, but doubts it will come.
