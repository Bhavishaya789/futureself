# The Demographic Structure of Indian Society
minutes: 40
yt: class 12 sociology demographic structure malthus demographic transition census 2011 sex ratio age structure literacy population policy
@study
Demography is the systematic study of population: its size, growth, composition and distribution. Thomas Malthus, in his 1798 Essay on the Principle of Population, argued that population grows geometrically while food grows arithmetically, so checks such as famine and disease limit growth. Karl Marx and others disagreed, blaming social organisation. The theory of demographic transition says societies move from high birth and death rates to low birth and death rates through stages, with a period of rapid growth in between. Key concepts are birth rate, death rate, rate of natural increase, fertility rate, infant mortality rate, maternal mortality ratio, life expectancy and sex ratio (females per 1000 males). The Census of India is conducted every ten years; the first complete synchronous census was in 1881, and the 2011 Census was the 15th. India’s population in 2011 was about 121 crore (1.21 billion), the second largest in the world; the sex ratio was 943, and the child sex ratio (0-6) fell to 919; the literacy rate was about 74 per cent; and the decadal growth was about 17.6 per cent. Kerala has the highest sex ratio and literacy, while Haryana has the lowest child sex ratio. Uttar Pradesh is the most populous state. The age structure of India is young, and the working age group of 15-59 years offers a demographic dividend, while the dependency ratio compares dependents to workers. Declining child sex ratio reflects son preference, sex-selective abortion and neglect of girls; the PCPNDT Act of 1994 bans prenatal sex determination. India launched the first official family planning programme in 1952, the National Population Policy in 2000 and emphasises health, education and women’s empowerment. Urbanisation has risen, with about 31 per cent living in towns in 2011. Population issues also include ageing, migration and regional variation.

Malthus: geometric versus arithmetic growth; demographic transition in stages.
