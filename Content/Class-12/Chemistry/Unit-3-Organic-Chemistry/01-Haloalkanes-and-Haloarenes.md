# Haloalkanes and Haloarenes
minutes: 45

@study
Haloalkanes contain a halogen attached to an sp3 carbon; haloarenes contain halogen on an sp2 carbon of an aromatic ring. Classification: mono, di, polyhalogen compounds; alkyl (primary, secondary, tertiary), allylic, benzylic, vinylic and aryl halides. Nomenclature: IUPAC names; e.g. CH3CH2Br is bromoethane. Nature of C-X bond: polar; bond length increases and bond enthalpy decreases from C-F to C-I. Preparation of haloalkanes: from alcohols with HX/ZnCl2 (Lucas reagent), PCl5, PCl3 or SOCl2 (best method as the by-products are gases); from hydrocarbons by free radical halogenation (gives mixtures); addition of HX to alkenes (Markovnikov's rule) and halogens; halogen exchange: Finkelstein reaction (alkyl chloride or bromide with NaI in dry acetone gives alkyl iodide) and Swarts reaction (alkyl chloride/bromide with AgF, Hg2F2, CoF2 or SbF3 gives alkyl fluoride). Haloarenes: electrophilic substitution with halogens in presence of Lewis acid; Sandmeyer reaction (diazonium salt with CuCl/HCl or CuBr/HBr); Gattermann reaction (Cu powder). Physical properties: boiling points are higher than hydrocarbons of comparable mass; they increase with mass of the halogen; branching lowers; insoluble in water; denser than water (chloro derivatives, bromo, iodo). Chemical reactions: nucleophilic substitution (SN) is the main reaction. SN2 (substitution nucleophilic bimolecular): single step, rate = k[RX][Nu], inversion of configuration (Walden inversion), favoured by primary alkyl halides and polar aprotic solvents; order of reactivity: methyl > primary > secondary > tertiary. SN1 (unimolecular): two steps; first slow step forms a carbocation; rate = k[RX]; racemisation (retention and inversion) with chiral substrates; favoured by tertiary, allylic and benzylic halides in polar protic solvents: tertiary > secondary > primary. Reactivity of halides: RI > RBr > RCl > RF. Chirality: a carbon with four different groups is a chiral (asymmetric) centre; enantiomers are non-superimposable mirror images, optically active; a racemic mixture (50:50) is optically inactive, called (+/-). Dextrorotatory (d or +) rotates the plane of polarised light to the right and laevorotatory (l or -) to the left. Elimination reactions: dehydrohalogenation with alcoholic KOH follows Saytzeff (Zaitsev) rule: the more substituted alkene is the major product. Reaction with aqueous KOH gives alcohols; alcoholic KOH gives alkenes. With metals: Grignard reagent RMgX is formed with Mg in dry ether; Wurtz reaction gives alkanes with sodium in dry ether (2RX + 2Na -> R-R + 2NaX); Wurtz-Fittig reaction gives alkylarenes. Haloarenes are less reactive towards nucleophilic substitution due to resonance (partial double bond character), sp2 hybridised carbon (shorter C-X bond) and instability of phenyl cation; they undergo nucleophilic substitution with strongly electron withdrawing groups at ortho or para positions (2,4-dinitrochlorobenzene reacts readily). Electrophilic substitution in haloarenes: halogens are deactivating but ortho-para directing. Fittig reaction gives biaryls. Polyhalogen compounds: dichloromethane (solvent; paint remover; harms the central nervous system), trichloromethane (chloroform; anaesthetic earlier; oxidised by air to poisonous phosgene so stored in dark bottles filled completely), triiodomethane (iodoform; antiseptic; yellow solid), tetrachloromethane (carbon tetrachloride; fire extinguisher; damages the liver and contributes to ozone depletion), freons (CFCs; refrigerants; ozone depletion) and DDT (dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane; first chlorinated organic insecticide; non-biodegradable and toxic to fish; banned in many countries).
## Deep dive: Substitution
SN2 gives inversion in one step; SN1 gives racemisation via a carbocation. Reactivity depends on structure and solvent.
## Deep dive: Preparation and elimination
Alkyl halides are made from alcohols or alkenes. Alcoholic KOH gives alkenes following Saytzeff rule.
## Deep dive: Haloarenes and polyhalogens
Haloarenes resist nucleophilic substitution unless activated. Freons and DDT cause environmental problems.
