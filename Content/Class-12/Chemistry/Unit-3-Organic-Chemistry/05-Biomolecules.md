@study
Biomolecules are the organic compounds that build and run living systems: carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, vitamins, hormones and enzymes. Carbohydrates are optically active polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones, or compounds that give these on hydrolysis; classified as monosaccharides (glucose, fructose, galactose; cannot be hydrolysed), oligosaccharides (disaccharides: sucrose, maltose, lactose) and polysaccharides (starch, cellulose, glycogen). Sugars are reducing (free aldehyde or ketone group, give Tollens and Fehling tests) or non-reducing (sucrose). Glucose C6H12O6 is an aldohexose; structure established by its reactions: acetylation with acetic anhydride shows five OH groups; oxidation with HNO3 gives saccharic acid (so it has a primary alcohol), with bromine water gives gluconic acid (so it has an aldehyde); HI gives n-hexane (straight chain of six carbons); HCN gives a cyanohydrin. It does not give Schiff's test and does not form a bisulphite addition product; the pentaacetate does not react with hydroxylamine; and glucose exists in two cyclic hemiacetal forms, alpha and beta anomers, differing at C-1 (the anomeric carbon); pyranose ring (six-membered) is formed by the C-1 aldehyde and C-5 OH. Mutarotation is the change in optical rotation on standing to a constant value of +52.5 degrees (alpha +111, beta +19.2). Glucose is D-(+)-glucose; the D and L descriptors refer to the configuration of the OH on the penultimate carbon relative to glyceraldehyde. Fructose is a ketohexose (D-(-)-fructose, furanose ring of five members). Sucrose is made of alpha-D-glucose and beta-D-fructose joined by a C1-C2 glycosidic link, hence non-reducing; it is dextrorotatory (+66.5), but on hydrolysis gives an equimolar mixture of glucose (+52.5) and fructose (-92.4), which is laevorotatory, hence the mixture is called invert sugar. Maltose has two alpha-D-glucose units joined by C1-C4 link (reducing). Lactose has beta-D-galactose and beta-D-glucose by a C1-C4 link (reducing, milk sugar). Starch has amylose (20 percent, water soluble, unbranched, alpha 1,4 links) and amylopectin (80 percent, insoluble, branched with alpha 1,4 and alpha 1,6 links). Cellulose is a straight chain of beta-D-glucose units with beta 1,4 links; humans cannot digest it. Glycogen is the animal starch stored in liver and muscles. Importance: energy source, cellulose in cell walls and paper, starch in food. Proteins are polymers of alpha-amino acids joined by peptide bonds (amide linkages). There are 20 standard amino acids; essential ones (valine, leucine, isoleucine, lysine, threonine, methionine, phenylalanine, tryptophan; histidine and arginine in children) cannot be made by the body; non-essential ones can. Amino acids exist as zwitterions (dipolar ions) in solution, are amphoteric, and have an isoelectric point where the net charge is zero and solubility is minimum. Glycine is the only optically inactive amino acid. Structures: primary (sequence of amino acids), secondary (alpha helix and beta pleated sheet held by hydrogen bonds), tertiary (folding by disulphide bridges, hydrogen bonds, ionic and van der Waals interactions) and quaternary (assembly of subunits, as in haemoglobin). Fibrous proteins (keratin, myosin) are insoluble; globular proteins (insulin, albumin) are soluble. Denaturation disrupts the secondary and tertiary structures but the primary structure remains, as in coagulation of egg white on boiling. Enzymes are biocatalysts, mostly globular proteins, specific and working at optimum temperature (about 310 K) and pH; they follow the lock-and-key model; examples are invertase, maltase, zymase, urease and pepsin; enzyme inhibitors compete for the active site. Vitamins are organic compounds required in small amounts: fat-soluble (A, D, E, K; stored in the body) and water-soluble (B group and C; need regular supply; excreted). Deficiency diseases: A (xerophthalmia, night blindness), B1 (beriberi), B2 (cheilosis), B6 (convulsions), B12 (pernicious anaemia), C (scurvy), D (rickets and osteomalacia), E (fragility of red blood cells), K (increased blood clotting time). Nucleic acids: DNA and RNA are polymers of nucleotides; a nucleotide has a nitrogenous base, a pentose sugar and a phosphate group; a nucleoside is base plus sugar. DNA has 2-deoxyribose and bases A, G, C, T; RNA has ribose and A, G, C, U. DNA is a double helix with A pairing with T by two hydrogen bonds and G with C by three. RNA is single stranded: mRNA, rRNA and tRNA. DNA carries heredity and replication; RNA performs protein synthesis. Hormones: insulin (peptide; lowers blood sugar), thyroxine (iodinated), steroid hormones (testosterone, oestrogen, cortisone), adrenaline.
## Deep dive: Carbohydrates
Glucose is an aldohexose with open and cyclic forms; sucrose is non-reducing and gives invert sugar. Starch is amylose plus amylopectin; cellulose has beta links.
## Deep dive: Proteins and enzymes
Amino acids link by peptide bonds. Proteins have four levels of structure; denaturation destroys secondary and tertiary structure. Enzymes are specific protein catalysts.
## Deep dive: Vitamins and nucleic acids
Vitamin deficiencies cause characteristic diseases. DNA and RNA differ in sugar and bases, and DNA pairs A-T and G-C.
