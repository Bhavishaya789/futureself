# Understanding Partition: Politics, Memories, Experiences
minutes: 45
yt: class 12 history theme 14 understanding partition muslim league lahore resolution direct action day radcliffe oral history
@study
The Partition of India in 1947 was a result of long-term and short-term developments. The Muslim League was founded in 1906. The idea of a separate state was articulated by Muhammad Iqbal in 1930, and the name Pakistan was coined by Choudhary Rahmat Ali in 1933. The two-nation theory was endorsed by the Lahore Resolution of 23 March 1940. The provincial elections of 1937 were a turning point: the Congress formed ministries in most provinces, and the League’s refusal of a coalition in the United Provinces deepened distrust. In the 1945-46 elections, the Congress won general seats and the League won most Muslim seats. The Cabinet Mission of 1946 proposed a loose federation, which was not accepted. The League called Direct Action Day on 16 August 1946, leading to violence in Calcutta. Violence also spread to Noakhali, Bihar and Punjab, and Gandhi went to Noakhali. Lord Mountbatten arrived as viceroy in March 1947, and the 3 June 1947 plan announced the division. The Indian Independence Act was passed on 18 July 1947. The boundaries were drawn by Cyril Radcliffe in a few weeks, and Punjab and Bengal were divided. Pakistan came into being on 14 August and India on 15 August. About fifteen million people were displaced and perhaps a million died. Women suffered abduction, and later recovery operations were controversial. Historians use oral history, letters, diaries, memoirs and fiction such as Saadat Hasan Manto’s stories alongside government records to study Partition. Oral testimony captures ordinary experiences but depends on memory.
## Deep dive: Causes
Communal politics, the 1937 elections, the Lahore Resolution and the failure of the Cabinet Mission led to division.
## Deep dive: Violence and displacement
Direct Action Day began a cycle of violence, and millions migrated across new borders.
## Deep dive: Sources
Oral histories, memoirs and fiction complement official records in understanding Partition.
