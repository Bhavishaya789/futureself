# Colonial Cities: Urbanisation, Planning and Architecture
minutes: 45
yt: class 12 history theme 12 colonial cities calcutta bombay madras new delhi lutyens baker
@study
Colonial cities grew out of trade settlements and administrative needs. Madras developed around Fort St George from 1639, Bombay from the seven islands given to Charles II as dowry by Portugal in 1661 and leased to the East India Company in 1668, and Calcutta from the village sites of Sutanuti, Kalikata and Govindpur, where Job Charnock settled in 1690 and Fort William was built. These three became Presidency capitals. Cities had a White Town and a Black Town, with the former built around forts. Calcutta’s Lottery Committee of 1817 planned drainage and roads, and Lord Wellesley built Government House. The railways, beginning with the Mumbai-Thane line in 1853, linked ports to hinterlands. Bombay grew as a cotton and shipping centre, housing mill workers in chawls, and the Bombay Improvement Trust was set up in 1898 after the plague of 1896. Census operations began, with the first all-India census in 1872 and regular decennial census from 1881, creating data on population. Hill stations such as Shimla and cantonments were created for British health and security. Public buildings in Gothic, neo-classical and Indo-Saracenic styles, such as the Victoria Terminus and Gateway of India, expressed imperial power. In 1911 at the Delhi Durbar, King George V announced the shift of the capital from Calcutta to Delhi. Edwin Lutyens and Herbert Baker designed New Delhi, which was inaugurated in 1931. Historians use maps, photographs, census records and municipal reports to study cities.
## Deep dive: Presidency cities
Madras, Bombay and Calcutta grew from trading posts into administrative and commercial centres.
## Deep dive: Urban planning
Drainage, municipalities, improvement trusts and segregation shaped colonial cities.
## Deep dive: Architecture and power
Gothic, neo-classical and Indo-Saracenic buildings, and New Delhi, displayed imperial authority.
