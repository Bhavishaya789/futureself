# Kings and Chronicles: The Mughal Courts
minutes: 45
yt: class 12 history theme 9 kings and chronicles mughal courts akbarnama baburnama sulh i kul
@study
The Mughal empire was founded after Babur defeated Ibrahim Lodi at Panipat in 1526. His successors were Humayun, Akbar (from 1556), Jahangir (1605), Shah Jahan (1628) and Aurangzeb (1658). Mughal rulers claimed descent from Timur on the father’s side and from Genghis Khan on the mother’s side, and their court culture used Persian as the language of administration. Chronicles were written to glorify the emperor. Key texts include the Baburnama, Akbarnama and Ain-i-Akbari by Abul Fazl, the Badshahnama by Abdul Hamid Lahori, the Jahangirnama, and the Humayunnama by Gulbadan Begum. Illustrated manuscripts were made in the taswir khana or painting workshop, with painters and calligraphers, using the nastaliq style. The court at Fatehpur Sikri, Agra and Shahjahanabad organised daily ritual: jharokha darshan, taslim, kornish and sijda. The emperor was seen as a source of light, as per Abul Fazl’s idea of divine kingship. Akbar followed the policy of sulh-i-kul or universal peace, held religious discussions at the Ibadat Khana from 1575, and brought nobles from varied backgrounds into the mansabdari system, where rank was marked by zat and sawar. Nobles included Iranis, Turanis, Rajputs and Indian Muslims. Imperial women, such as Nur Jahan, Jahanara and Gulbadan Begum, played roles in politics and patronage. Provincial administration used subas under subedars, with officials such as diwan, bakhshi and qazi. Shah Jahan built the Red Fort and Taj Mahal, and Aurangzeb introduced the jizya in 1679. Diplomacy included embassies like that of Sir Thomas Roe at Jahangir’s court.
## Deep dive: Chronicles and court
Persian chronicles and paintings projected an ideal image of rulers; historians read them critically.
## Deep dive: Akbar’s policies
Sulh-i-kul, the Ibadat Khana and the mansabdari system aimed to integrate diverse nobles and communities.
## Deep dive: Court life
Ceremonies like jharokha darshan and taslim showed imperial authority and accessibility.
