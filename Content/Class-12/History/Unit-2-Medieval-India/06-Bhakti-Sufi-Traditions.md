# Bhakti-Sufi Traditions: Changes in Religious Beliefs and Devotional Texts
minutes: 45
yt: class 12 history theme 6 bhakti sufi traditions kabir nanak mirabai chishti
@study
Between roughly the eighth and eighteenth centuries, devotional movements spread across the subcontinent. In the Tamil region, the Alvars devoted to Vishnu and the Nayanars devoted to Shiva composed hymns; women like Andal and Karaikkal Ammaiyar were among them. Basavanna founded the Virashaiva or Lingayat tradition in Karnataka in the twelfth century, rejecting caste and ritual pollution. Saguna bhakti worshipped deities with form, as in the Ramcharitmanas of Tulsidas and the songs of Mirabai and Surdas, while nirguna bhakti, as taught by Kabir, saw the divine as formless. Kabir, a weaver associated with Banaras, criticised ritual and sectarian divisions; his verses are compiled in the Bijak and the Kabir Granthavali. Guru Nanak, born in 1469 at Talwandi, founded Sikhism, taught one God, and set up a community at Kartarpur. The Adi Granth was compiled by Guru Arjan in 1604, and Guru Gobind Singh founded the Khalsa in 1699. In western India, Namdev and Tukaram sang of Vithoba. Sufism, the mystical tradition of Islam, spread through silsilas or orders such as the Chishti and Suhrawardi. Sufis lived in khanqahs, and their tombs, dargahs, became centres of pilgrimage. Moinuddin Chishti at Ajmer, Nizamuddin Auliya at Delhi and Baba Farid are notable. Sufi poetry, qawwali and local languages helped spread ideas. Historians study these movements through hagiographies, poetry and inscriptions, noticing exchange between traditions rather than a simple division.
## Deep dive: Bhakti movements
Alvars, Nayanars, Basavanna, Kabir, Nanak and Mirabai show diverse forms of devotion across regions.
## Deep dive: Sufi silsilas
Sufi orders such as the Chishti created khanqahs and dargahs that became centres of devotion.
## Deep dive: Exchange of ideas
Bhakti and Sufi traditions influenced each other through poetry, language and shared saint worship.
