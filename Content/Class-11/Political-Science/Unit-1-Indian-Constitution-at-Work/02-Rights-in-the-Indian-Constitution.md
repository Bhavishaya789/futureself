Fundamental Rights are in Part III (Articles 12 to 35) and are enforceable by courts. The six fundamental rights are: the Right to Equality (Articles 14 to 18), the Right to Freedom (Articles 19 to 22), the Right against Exploitation (Articles 23 and 24), the Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25 to 28), Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29 and 30) and the Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32). Article 14 guarantees equality before law and equal protection of laws; Article 15 prohibits discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth; Article 16 gives equality of opportunity in public employment; Article 17 abolishes untouchability; Article 18 abolishes titles. Article 19 gives six freedoms: speech and expression, assembly, association, movement, residence and profession. Article 20 protects against conviction for offences; Article 21 protects life and personal liberty; Article 21A (86th Amendment, 2002) makes education compulsory for children aged 6 to 14; Article 22 protects against arbitrary arrest. Article 23 prohibits trafficking and forced labour; Article 24 prohibits employment of children below 14 in hazardous work. Article 25 gives freedom of conscience and religion. Article 29 protects the interests of minorities and Article 30 gives minorities the right to establish educational institutions. Article 32, called by Ambedkar the heart and soul of the Constitution, lets a person move the Supreme Court; writs are habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari and quo warranto; High Courts issue writs under Article 226. The right to property was removed from fundamental rights by the 44th Amendment in 1978 and is now a legal right under Article 300A. Rights may be restricted by reasonable restrictions in public interest, and can be suspended in an emergency (except Articles 20 and 21). Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV, Articles 36 to 51) are guidelines for the state to build a welfare state; they are non-justiciable. Fundamental Duties (Part IV-A, Article 51A) were added by the 42nd Amendment in 1976 on the Swaran Singh Committee and number 11 after the 86th Amendment added a duty of parents to educate children. The Kesavananda Bharati case (1973) established the basic structure doctrine; Maneka Gandhi (1978) widened Article 21. The National Human Rights Commission was set up in 1993 under the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993. The Right to Information Act, 2005 and the Right to Education Act, 2009 expanded rights.
## Deep dive: Fundamental rights
Rights protect individuals against the state. Equality removes discrimination. Freedom includes speech but with reasonable limits. Remedies make rights effective.
## Deep dive: Directive Principles and duties
DPSP aim at social and economic democracy, including equal pay, living wage, village panchayats and uniform civil code (Article 44). Duties remind citizens to respect the Constitution, protect the environment and promote harmony.
## Deep dive: Interpretation
Courts have widened Article 21 to include privacy, education, health and livelihood. The Puttaswamy case (2017) held privacy a fundamental right. Writs protect rights quickly.
