# Constitution as a Living Document
minutes: 40
yt: class 11 political science constitution as a living document amendment basic structure
@study
A living document adapts to changing needs while preserving basic values. The Indian Constitution has provision for its own amendment under Article 368. There are three methods: some provisions can be amended by a simple majority of Parliament (such as the formation of new states, citizenship, Rajya Sabha seats); most provisions need a special majority, which is a majority of the total membership of each House and at least two-thirds of members present and voting; and federal provisions (election of the President, the Union-state distribution of powers, the Supreme Court and High Courts, representation of states in Parliament) need a special majority plus ratification by at least half of the state legislatures. The President must give assent to an amendment bill. The Constitution was amended for the first time in 1951 (First Amendment, adding the Ninth Schedule and restrictions on freedom of speech). Important amendments: 7th (1956, reorganisation of states); 24th and 25th (1971); 42nd (1976, mini-constitution: added socialist, secular, integrity, fundamental duties); 44th (1978, removed the right to property as fundamental right); 52nd (1985, anti-defection); 61st (1988, voting age 18); 73rd and 74th (1992, local government); 86th (2002, right to education); 91st (2003, size of the council of ministers); 101st (2016, GST); 103rd (2019, 10 percent reservation for the economically weaker sections); 104th (2019); 106th (2023, women reservation). The basic structure doctrine: in Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala (1973) the Supreme Court held that Parliament can amend any part but cannot destroy the basic structure; earlier in Golaknath (1967) it had said Parliament cannot amend fundamental rights; Minerva Mills (1980) reaffirmed the basic structure; S R Bommai (1994) held secularism part of the basic structure and limited the use of Article 356. Basic features include supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic government, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, judicial review, free and fair elections and the rule of law. Amendments reflect political consensus, judicial interpretation and social change. The Constitution has stood the test of time because it is flexible but protects core values; some amendments were criticised, such as the 42nd and the 39th. The Constitution is also altered by judicial interpretation and conventions.
## Deep dive: Amendment procedure
Simple majority for minor matters, special majority for most and ratification for federal provisions. Unlike some countries, no referendum is needed. State legislatures cannot initiate an amendment.
## Deep dive: Basic structure
The doctrine limits Parliament's power. It protects democracy, rights and the rule of law from being destroyed by temporary majorities. The court decides what constitutes basic structure.
## Deep dive: Change by interpretation
Courts expand meaning, as with Article 21. Conventions such as the PM being the Lok Sabha leader develop. Political practice, coalitions and judicial review shape the working of the Constitution.
