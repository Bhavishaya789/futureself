# Writing and City Life

@study
Mesopotamia means the land between rivers: the Tigris and the Euphrates in present-day Iraq. Here around 3000 BCE the first cities appeared in Sumer, such as Uruk, Ur and Nippur. The Sumerians invented cuneiform writing (wedge-shaped signs on clay tablets) around 3200 BCE for recording temple accounts. Cities grew because of agriculture on irrigated land, trade with distant lands like Dilmun (Bahrain), Magan (Oman) and Meluhha (the Indus region), and the organised power of temples. Temples (ziggurats) were centres of economy and religion. Kings such as Sargon of Akkad (about 2370 BCE) created the first empire. Hammurabi of Babylon (about 1792 to 1750 BCE) issued a famous law code. The Assyrian king Ashurbanipal built a library at Nineveh. Mesopotamians developed the wheel, the plough, bronze, a calendar and a sexagesimal (base 60) number system. The Epic of Gilgamesh is among the oldest literary works. Leonard Woolley excavated the royal tombs of Ur.
## Deep dive: City life
Uruk was the earliest city, with temples, craftsmen and traders. Cities needed rulers, surplus food and specialists. Irrigation canals and flood control required co-operation. Urban society had priests, scribes, merchants, craftsmen, farmers and slaves.
## Deep dive: Writing
Writing began as pictographs and became cuneiform on clay tablets with a reed stylus. Scribes were trained in schools called tablet houses. Writing helped record trade, law, astronomy and literature. Cuneiform was used for several languages across West Asia.
## Deep dive: Empires and legacy
Akkadian, Babylonian and Assyrian empires followed. Hammurabi's code reflected social order and punishment. Mesopotamian science influenced later Greek learning. The 60-minute hour and 360-degree circle trace to Mesopotamia.
