# The Central Islamic Lands

@study
Islam arose in Arabia in the seventh century CE through the Prophet Muhammad (570 to 632 CE). In 622 CE the Prophet migrated from Mecca to Medina (the Hijra), which marks the beginning of the Islamic calendar. After his death the community was led by the Rashidun caliphs: Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman and Ali. The Umayyad dynasty (661 to 750 CE), with its capital at Damascus, expanded the empire to Spain in the west and Sind in the east; the battle of Karbala in 680 CE, in which Husayn was killed, caused a lasting split between Sunni and Shia communities. The Abbasids (750 to 1258 CE) overthrew the Umayyads and built Baghdad as their capital. They patronised learning at the House of Wisdom, where Greek, Persian and Indian works were translated. Scholars like Al-Khwarizmi (algebra), Al-Razi (medicine), Ibn Sina (Avicenna), Ibn Rushd (Averroes), Ibn Khaldun (history) and Al-Biruni advanced knowledge. The Fatimids ruled Egypt, the Seljuq Turks took over Baghdad in 1055, and the Crusades (1095 to 1291) brought conflict with Christian Europe; Saladin retook Jerusalem in 1187. The Mongols under Hulagu destroyed Baghdad in 1258.
## Deep dive: Early Islam and caliphate
The Prophet formed a community (umma) in Medina. The caliph was the successor and leader. Conquests spread Islam across West Asia and North Africa. The empire used Arabic as an administrative language and issued coins.
## Deep dive: Dynasties
Umayyads: Arab aristocracy, Damascus, administrative reforms by Abd al-Malik. Abbasids: Persian influence, Baghdad, Al-Mamun and Harun al-Rashid. Fatimids: Shia dynasty in Cairo that founded Al-Azhar. Seljuqs: Turkish power. Mongols ended the Abbasid caliphate in 1258.
## Deep dive: Culture and economy
Cities like Baghdad, Cordoba and Cairo became centres of learning. Mathematics, astronomy, medicine and philosophy flourished. Trade networks connected the Indian Ocean and the Mediterranean. Mosques, madrasas and libraries spread learning.
