# Units and Measurements

@study
Physics is based on measurement. A physical quantity has a numerical value and a unit. Fundamental (base) quantities in the SI system are length (metre, m), mass (kilogram, kg), time (second, s), electric current (ampere, A), temperature (kelvin, K), amount of substance (mole, mol) and luminous intensity (candela, cd). Supplementary units are the radian (plane angle) and steradian (solid angle). Derived units come from base units, for example newton (kg m s^-2) and joule (kg m^2 s^-2). The SI system was adopted in 1971 and is based on seven base units. Dimensions are the powers of base quantities in a derived quantity: [M], [L], [T]. Examples: velocity [L T^-1], acceleration [L T^-2], force [M L T^-2], work and energy [M L^2 T^-2], power [M L^2 T^-3], pressure [M L^-1 T^-2]. Uses of dimensional analysis: checking the correctness of an equation, deriving relations between quantities and converting units. Limitations: it cannot find dimensionless constants and cannot handle trigonometric or exponential functions. The principle of homogeneity says every term in a correct equation has the same dimensions. Significant figures are the reliable digits in a measurement. Rules: all non-zero digits are significant; zeros between non-zero digits are significant; leading zeros are not; trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant. In multiplication or division the result has as many significant figures as the quantity with the fewest; in addition or subtraction it keeps the least number of decimal places. Errors: the absolute error is the difference between the measured and true value; mean absolute error is the average of absolute errors; relative error is the ratio of mean absolute error to the mean value; percentage error is relative error times 100. Systematic errors (instrumental, personal, environmental) shift all readings; random errors vary. Combination of errors: in addition and subtraction absolute errors add; in multiplication and division relative errors add; for x to the power n the relative error is n times that of x. Least count of a vernier caliper is 0.1 mm (1 main scale division minus 1 vernier division) and of a screw gauge is pitch divided by number of divisions on the circular scale, usually 0.01 mm. Accuracy refers to closeness to the true value and precision to closeness among measurements. Orders of magnitude: 1 light year = 9.46 x 10^15 m; 1 astronomical unit = 1.496 x 10^11 m; 1 parsec = 3.08 x 10^16 m; 1 atomic mass unit = 1.66 x 10^-27 kg.
## Deep dive: Units and dimensions
SI is a coherent system. Dimensions help check formulas. For example the formula for time period T = 2 pi sqrt(l/g) has dimension [T] on both sides. Dimensional analysis gives only the form of a relation.
## Deep dive: Significant figures
Counting significant figures shows precision. 0.00250 has three. 2.50 x 10^3 has three. Rounding follows standard rules: drop digits below 5 and raise if above 5.
## Deep dive: Errors and instruments
Errors add differently for sums and products. Vernier calipers and screw gauges give precise readings using the least count. Zero error must be corrected.
