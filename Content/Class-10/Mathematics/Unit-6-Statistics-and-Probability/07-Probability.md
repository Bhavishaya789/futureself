# Probability
minutes: 35
yt: class 10 maths probability
@study
Probability measures how likely an event is. For equally likely outcomes, P(E) = number of favourable outcomes / total number of outcomes. It always lies between 0 and 1: 0 for an impossible event and 1 for a sure event. The sum of the probabilities of all elementary outcomes of an experiment is 1, and P(E) + P(not E) = 1. Typical experiments are tossing coins, throwing dice, drawing cards from a pack of 52 and picking balls from a bag.
## Deep dive: Classical definition
P(E) = favourable / total. Outcomes must be equally likely. For one coin the sample space is {H, T}; for one die it is {1,2,3,4,5,6}; for two dice there are 36 outcomes; for two coins 4; for three coins 8.
## Deep dive: Complementary events
The complement of E is not E, with P(not E) = 1 - P(E). It is the quickest route for questions with the words at least, at most or not. For example P(at least one head in two tosses) = 1 - P(TT) = 3/4.
## Deep dive: Playing cards
A pack has 52 cards: 4 suits (spades, clubs black; hearts, diamonds red) of 13 cards each. There are 12 face cards (J, Q, K), 4 aces, 26 red and 26 black cards. Remember that the probability of a king is 4/52 = 1/13.
## Deep dive: Two dice
The sample space has 36 pairs. Sum 7 has 6 ways, the most likely sum. Sum 2 and 12 have 1 way each. A doublet (same number) has 6 outcomes. Number of ways for sum s is 6 - |s - 7|.

Q: Define probability of an event.
A: P(E) = number of outcomes favourable to E divided by the total number of equally likely outcomes of the experiment.
Q: Why can probability never exceed 1?
A: Favourable outcomes cannot be more than the total outcomes, so the ratio is at most 1; it equals 1 only for a sure event.
Q: What does a probability of 0 mean?
A: The event can never happen. For example getting 7 on a single die.
Q: State the complementary event rule.
A: P(E) + P(not E) = 1. So P(not E) = 1 - P(E).
Q: How do you handle at least one questions?
A: Compute the probability of none and subtract from 1. For two coins, P(at least one head) = 1 - 1/4 = 3/4.
Q: Write the sample space of tossing two coins.
A: {HH, HT, TH, TT}, four equally likely outcomes.
Q: How many cards are in a pack and how are they divided?
A: 52 cards in 4 suits of 13: spades and clubs are black, hearts and diamonds are red. Each suit has A, 2 to 10, J, Q, K.
Q: Are the outcomes of a biased coin equally likely?
A: No. The classical formula applies only to equally likely outcomes.
Q: Difference between experimental and theoretical probability?
A: Experimental probability comes from repeated trials; theoretical comes from reasoning with equally likely outcomes. Class 10 deals with the theoretical type.
Q: Why is the sum 7 most likely with two dice?
A: It has 6 favourable pairs out of 36, more than any other sum.

## Moderate
P: A bag has 5 red, 3 blue and 2 green balls. One ball is drawn at random. Find the probability that it is not blue.
A: Total = 10. P(blue) = 3/10 so P(not blue) = 1 - 3/10 = 7/10.

## High
P: Two dice are thrown together. Find the probability that the sum is a prime number.
A: Total 36. Prime sums: 2 (1 way), 3 (2), 5 (4), 7 (6), 11 (2) = 15 ways. P = 15/36 = 5/12.
R: 2 | Total 36
R: 2 | Prime sums listed
R: 2 | Counts 15
R: 2 | P = 5/12
## Advanced
P: A card is drawn from a well shuffled pack of 52 cards. Find the probability that it is neither a king nor a red card.
A: Red cards 26 and kings 4, of which 2 are red, so red or king = 26 + 2 = 28. Neither = 52 - 28 = 24. P = 24/52 = 6/13.
R: 2 | Count red or king without double counting
R: 2 | Subtract from 52
R: 2 | P = 24/52
R: 2 | Simplify to 6/13
