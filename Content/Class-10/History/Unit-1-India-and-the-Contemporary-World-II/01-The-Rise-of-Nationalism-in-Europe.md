# The Rise of Nationalism in Europe
minutes: 45

@study
Nationalism in Europe grew from the French Revolution of 1789, which gave Europe the idea of a nation of citizens with equal rights, a flag, a common language and one set of laws. After Napoleon fell, the Congress of Vienna (1815) tried to restore old monarchies, so liberals and nationalists worked through secret societies and revolutions (1830, 1848). Italy was unified under Cavour, Garibaldi and King Victor Emmanuel II (1861, Rome 1871), and Germany under Bismarck and Prussia (1871). Nationalism also grew through culture: folk songs, language, art and female symbols such as Marianne and Germania. In the Balkans it mixed with imperial rivalry and led towards the First World War.
## Deep dive: the French Revolution and the idea of the nation
In 1789 France was ruled by a monarch. The revolution transferred sovereignty to the people. La patrie (the fatherland) and le citoyen (the citizen) were introduced, the royal standard gave way to the tricolour, the Estates General became the National Assembly, new hymns were composed, oaths taken and martyrs commemorated. A central administrative system made uniform laws for all citizens, internal customs duties were abolished, weights and measures were standardised and French became the common national language while regional dialects were discouraged.
## Deep dive: Napoleon and the Civil Code of 1804
Napoleon destroyed democracy in France and made himself emperor, but in the administrative field he made the system rational and efficient. The Civil Code of 1804, also called the Napoleonic Code, did away with all privileges based on birth, established equality before the law and secured the right to property. He simplified administrative divisions, abolished the feudal system and freed peasants from serfdom and manorial dues. Guild restrictions were removed in towns, and transport and communication improved. Yet in Holland, Switzerland, Italy and Germany, people who first welcomed the French soon turned hostile because of higher taxes, censorship and forced conscription into the French armies.
## Deep dive: liberal nationalism and the Zollverein
Liberalism comes from the Latin liber, free. For the new middle classes it meant freedom for the individual and equality before the law; politically it meant government by consent, a constitution and representative government, but voting rights were limited to property-owning men. Economically it meant freedom of markets and the end of state restrictions on the movement of goods and capital. In 1834 Prussia started a customs union, the Zollverein, which abolished tariff barriers and reduced the number of currencies from over thirty to two, strengthening the idea of a German economic nation.
## Deep dive: conservatism, the Congress of Vienna and the revolutions
After Napoleon was defeated in 1815, European powers (Britain, Russia, Prussia and Austria) met at Vienna under the Austrian chancellor Duke Metternich. They restored the Bourbon dynasty in France, set up a buffer kingdom of the Netherlands including Belgium, added Genoa to Piedmont, gave Prussia territory on its western frontier and Austria northern Italy, and left the German Confederation of 39 states untouched. Conservative regimes were autocratic, tolerated no criticism and imposed censorship. Liberal-nationalists formed secret societies such as the Carbonari. Mazzini founded Young Italy and Young Europe. The July Revolution of 1830 in France and the 1848 revolt of liberals, workers and artisans shook the old order.

Q: How did the July Revolution of 1830 affect Europe?
A: The Bourbon kings of France were overthrown and Louis Philippe headed a constitutional monarchy. Metternich's remark that when France sneezes the rest of Europe catches cold showed how it encouraged revolt in Belgium, which broke away from the Netherlands.
Q: Why did the Frankfurt Parliament fail?
A: It offered a constitutional monarchy to the Prussian king, who rejected the crown with the support of the aristocracy and army. The middle-class dominated assembly also ignored workers and women, so it lost support and troops dispersed it.
Q: What role did women play in the liberal movements of 1848?
A: Many women formed political associations, founded newspapers and took part in meetings, but they were not given the right to vote and were only allowed to watch the Frankfurt Parliament from the visitors' gallery.
Q: Why did Napoleon's reforms get a mixed reception outside France?
A: The reforms ended feudalism and gave equality before law, so many welcomed them. But taxes, censorship and forced conscription for French wars made the people see the French as invaders.
Q: How did the middle class shape liberal nationalism?
A: Industrialisation created a new class of businessmen and professionals who wanted freedom of markets, equality before law and constitutions, and they supported national unification to expand trade.
Q: What was the significance of the Zollverein?
A: It abolished tariff barriers among German states and cut the number of currencies from over thirty to two. This created economic unity and strengthened feelings of belonging to one nation.

## Moderate
P: Explain how the French Revolution spread the idea of a nation to the people of France.
A: The revolution moved sovereignty from the king to the citizens (la patrie, le citoyen). The tricolour replaced the royal standard, the Estates General became the National Assembly, hymns, oaths and martyrs created a shared identity, a central administration made uniform laws, internal customs duties were abolished, weights and measures were standardised, and French became the common language, with regional dialects discouraged.

## High
P: Describe the main features of Napoleon's Civil Code of 1804 and explain why people outside France later turned against French rule.
A: The Civil Code abolished privileges based on birth, established equality before the law and protected the right to property. It also simplified administration, ended feudal dues and serfdom, removed guild restrictions and improved transport and communication. Outside France, early welcome turned to hostility because of rising taxes, censorship and forced conscription, which showed that the French armies were often seen as an occupying force.

## Advanced
P: Compare the unification of Italy and Germany: name the leaders, the states that led it and the methods used.
A: Italy was divided into seven states; Sardinia-Piedmont, ruled by Victor Emmanuel II, led unification. Cavour used diplomacy and an alliance with France to defeat Austria in 1859, while Garibaldi's Red Shirts conquered the Two Sicilies in 1860; Victor Emmanuel was proclaimed king in 1861 and Rome joined in 1871. Germany was united under Prussia: Bismarck, the Prussian chief minister, relied on the army and bureaucracy and won three wars in seven years against Denmark, Austria and France. In January 1871 William I was proclaimed German Emperor at Versailles. Both were led by a strong monarchic state, not by the liberal revolutions of 1848.

R: 2 | Methods: diplomacy and volunteers in Italy, army and war in Germany
R: 2 | Conclusion that monarchic states led both unifications
