# The Making of a Global World
minutes: 40
yt: class 10 history the making of a global world
@study
The global world grew from ancient silk routes and the exchange of food, goods and ideas. After 1492 the Americas joined the world economy and silver, disease and slavery changed the world. In the nineteenth century trade, migration and capital flows expanded under the influence of the abolition of the Corn Laws, railways, steamships and colonial rule, and Indians were sent as indentured labour. The First World War, the 1920s boom and the Great Depression (1929) wrecked this economy. After the Second World War the Bretton Woods Conference (1944) created the IMF and World Bank, decolonisation brought new nations, and from the 1970s multinational companies, floating exchange rates and a shift of industry to Asia led to a new phase of globalisation.
## Deep dive: the pre-modern world
The silk routes linked Asia with Europe and North Africa, carrying Chinese pottery, textiles from India and spices from Southeast Asia, while Christian missionaries, Muslim preachers and Buddhist monks travelled the same roads. Food also travelled: noodles are believed to have gone from China to the West, and potatoes, soya, groundnuts, maize, tomatoes, chillies and sweet potatoes reached the rest of the world after the Americas were discovered in 1492. The potato became the staple of poor Europeans; the Irish potato famine of the 1840s killed about a million people. After the conquest by the Portuguese and Spanish in the sixteenth century, smallpox and other diseases killed native Americans, and silver from Potosi in Peru and Mexico financed trade with Asia. African slaves were forced to work on cotton and sugar plantations.
## Deep dive: the nineteenth century world economy
Britain abolished the Corn Laws in 1846 under pressure from industrialists and urban people, which opened the country to cheap food imports and led farmers to fall back. Railways, steamships and the telegraph speeded the movement of goods and people, and refrigerated ships allowed the shipment of meat. In Africa, the cattle plague rinderpest of the 1890s killed about 90 percent of cattle, which forced Africans to take wage work. Indentured labourers from India, who signed contracts for five years, went to plantations in the Caribbean, Fiji, Mauritius and Trinidad, and the system was abolished in 1921. India supplied raw cotton, jute and wheat, and paid Britain home charges while its exports earned a surplus that balanced Britain's deficits with other countries.
## Deep dive: war, boom and depression
The First World War of 1914-18 was fought between the Allies (Britain, France, Russia) and the Central Powers (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Ottoman Turkey), caused about 9 million deaths and turned the USA from a debtor into a creditor. In the 1920s the American economy boomed with Ford's assembly line, mass production and consumer goods bought on credit, but agriculture suffered overproduction. The Great Depression started in 1929 after the Wall Street Crash, when banks recalled loans, farm prices collapsed, thousands of banks failed and unemployment reached about one third of the US workforce by 1933. Indian exports and prices of farm goods fell sharply, so peasants fell into debt.
## Deep dive: the post-war order
In July 1944 delegates from 44 countries met at Bretton Woods in New Hampshire, USA. They set up the International Monetary Fund to deal with external surpluses and deficits and the World Bank (IBRD) to finance post-war reconstruction, with fixed exchange rates linked to the dollar and gold. Many former colonies became independent but remained poor, so the G-77 demanded a New International Economic Order in the 1960s. From the 1970s exchange rates were floated, multinational companies (MNCs) moved production to low-wage Asian countries and industrial unemployment rose in the West. The 1990s saw China, India and Brazil among the fast-growing economies.

Q: How did the silk routes link the world?
A: Merchants carried Chinese pottery, Indian textiles and Southeast Asian spices across Asia to Europe and Africa. Travellers also spread religions such as Buddhism, Christianity and Islam along these routes.
Q: Why did the potato become important in Europe?
A: It gave poor people a cheap, filling food that grew well in poor soil, improving diet and life expectancy, but heavy dependence led to the Irish famine when blight destroyed the crop in the 1840s.
Q: How did disease help the conquest of the Americas?
A: The native Americans had no immunity to smallpox brought by Europeans. Millions died, which weakened resistance and helped the Spanish and Portuguese conquer them.
Q: Why did Britain abolish the Corn Laws?
A: Industrial growth raised food demand. Industrialists and city dwellers wanted cheaper food, and under their pressure the government abolished the laws, allowing food imports that undercut British farmers.
Q: What were the effects of rinderpest in Africa?
A: It killed about 90 percent of cattle, destroying livelihoods. Africans had to work for wages on mines and plantations, which helped colonisers conquer and control the continent.
Q: Why did people migrate in the nineteenth century?
A: People left for jobs and better lives. Industry and farms in the Americas and Australia needed workers, and cheaper ships and railways made travel easier, while many Indians went under indenture.
Q: Describe the life of Indian indentured labourers.
A: They signed contracts, often under false promises, and worked on plantations in the Caribbean, Fiji and Mauritius. Conditions were harsh, and many could not return after the contract ended.
Q: How did indentured labourers create new cultures?
A: They developed cultural forms such as the Hosay festival, chutney music in Trinidad and Guyana, and the Rastafarian movement.
Q: What was India's role in the nineteenth-century world economy?
A: India exported raw cotton, jute and wheat and imported British manufactures. Its trade surplus with Britain helped pay Britain's deficits with other countries and home charges.
Q: How did the First World War change the global economy?
A: War debts made the US a creditor and Britain a debtor. Industries in Europe were shifted to war production and trade was disrupted, hurting economies for years.
Q: What was the effect of mass production in the USA?
A: Ford's assembly line lowered costs and raised sales of cars, refrigerators and washing machines. Credit financed consumers, but demand finally slowed and left overproduction.
Q: Why did the Great Depression spread across the world?
A: US banks stopped lending and recalled loans, and countries raised tariffs. Falling demand and prices cut trade and production and spread unemployment.
Q: How did the Great Depression affect India?
A: Exports fell, prices of farm goods dropped by half while revenue stayed fixed, peasants went into debt, and gold flowed out of India, benefiting Britain.
Q: What decisions were taken at Bretton Woods?
A: The IMF was set up to handle external deficits and surpluses, the World Bank to finance reconstruction, and exchange rates were fixed relative to the dollar and gold.
Q: Why were the IMF and World Bank called Bretton Woods twins?
A: Both were created at the 1944 conference and were meant to work together to stabilise the post-war international economic system.
Q: What was the NIEO?
A: The New International Economic Order was a demand of developing countries, organised as the G-77, for control over their natural resources and fairer trade terms.
Q: How did MNCs change the world economy after 1970?
A: They moved production to low-wage countries in Asia to cut costs, which created jobs there but caused industrial unemployment in the West.
Q: Why did fixed exchange rates end?
A: US finances and competition from Europe and Japan weakened the dollar, so by the 1970s the system of fixed rates broke and floating rates came in.
Q: What is globalisation according to the chapter?
A: It is the growing integration of the world through the flow of trade, people, capital and ideas, a process with several phases.
Q: How did the Depression show the danger of a connected economy?
A: A crash in one country spread to others through trade and credit, so problems in the US banking system affected farmers in India and workers in Europe.

## Moderate
P: Explain how the discovery of the Americas changed the world economy.
A: After 1492 food crops such as potato, maize and tomato spread across the world, improving diets and population. The conquest by Spain and Portugal and the use of silver from Potosi and Mexico financed trade with Asia, and the demand for labour on plantations led to the slave trade, while diseases like smallpox destroyed the native population.

## High
P: Describe how the Great Depression began and how it affected India.
A: The depression began in 1929 with overproduction in agriculture, falling prices and the Wall Street Crash. US banks recalled loans from Europe, causing bank failures, collapse of trade and mass unemployment. In India exports of jute and cotton fell, prices of wheat and jute dropped, and peasants were hit by rigid revenue demands and debt.

R: 2 | Banks recalling loans and unemployment
R: 2 | Fall in Indian exports and prices
R: 2 | Peasants' debt
## Advanced
P: Discuss the role of the Bretton Woods institutions and why their system changed after 1970.
A: Bretton Woods in 1944 created the IMF to handle balance of payments and the World Bank to finance reconstruction, with fixed exchange rates linked to the dollar and gold. It supported growth in the West in the 1950s and 1960s. By the 1970s US finances weakened, fixed rates collapsed and floating exchange rates were introduced. Developing countries could not rely on the IMF and World Bank alone and demanded a New International Economic Order, while MNCs shifted production to low-wage countries in Asia, ending an era of Western industrial dominance.
R: 2 | IMF, World Bank and fixed exchange rates
R: 2 | Growth in the 1950s and 1960s
R: 2 | Collapse of fixed rates and floating system
R: 2 | G-77, NIEO and shift of MNCs to Asia
