# The Age of Industrialisation
minutes: 40
yt: class 10 history the age of industrialisation
@study
Industrialisation did not begin with factories alone. Before them, merchants used proto-industrialisation, giving work to peasants and artisans in the countryside. In Britain, cotton mills, Arkwright, Watt's steam engine and the railways drove the Industrial Revolution, yet hand labour remained important because it was cheap and seasonal. In India, textiles were exported from Surat, Masulipatam and Hooghly until the East India Company's gomasthas, monopoly and the flood of Manchester goods ruined many weavers. From 1854 Indian entrepreneurs such as Dwarkanath Tagore, the Tatas, Birla and Hukumchand set up cotton, jute and steel firms, helped by the First World War and Swadeshi. Advertisements and labels created a market, and labour was recruited through jobbers.
## Deep dive: before the factory: proto-industrialisation
From the seventeenth century merchants from towns moved to the countryside, supplying money to peasants and artisans to produce goods for an international market. Poor peasants and cottagers welcomed this, as it gave extra income. Producers worked at home, while the clothier bought wool from the stapler, then gave it to spinners, carders and fullers before it reached the weavers. This was called proto-industrialisation because it was a phase before and alongside the factory. It was also more profitable for the merchants because they escaped the restrictions of urban guilds.
## Deep dive: the coming of the factory
The first factories in England came up by the 1730s, and the cotton mill became the symbol of the new era. Richard Arkwright created the cotton mill, and new machines raised output: the spinning jenny of James Hargreaves (1764), steam engines improved by James Watt, and rail. Raw cotton imports rose from 2.5 million pounds in 1760 to 22 million pounds in 1787. Yet hand labour did not vanish. Many industries, such as Victorian tailoring, bookbinding and the London season trades, relied on seasonal demand and cheap labour. Workers feared machines, and women attacked spinning jennies in the woollen industry.
## Deep dive: Indian textiles and colonial rule
Before the 1760s Indian silk and cotton goods dominated trade in Asia. Surat, Masulipatam and Hooghly were major ports; with colonial control, Bombay and Calcutta rose. The East India Company wanted a monopoly, eliminated competition, appointed paid gomasthas to supervise weavers, checked the quality of cloth and prevented weavers from selling to others. Weavers rebelled in places. By the 1850s Manchester cloth flooded Indian markets, raw cotton exports rose and weavers lost both markets and raw materials. In the 1860s the American Civil War cut the cotton supply to Britain, so Indian cotton prices rose and weavers were starved of supply.
## Deep dive: Indian factories, workers and markets
The first cotton mill in Bombay started in 1854 and the first jute mill in Bengal in 1855. Dwarkanath Tagore invested in shipping, mining and plantations, while the Parsis Dinshaw Petit and Jamsetji Tata, the Marwari Seth Hukumchand and the Birla family built industries. Tata Iron and Steel at Jamshedpur began producing steel in 1912. The First World War cut British imports and created demand for jute bags, cloth and steel. Mill workers were hired through jobbers who also controlled them. New markets were built with advertisements, calendars and labels, and Indian firms used nationalist messages. Small handloom units survived by using fly-shuttle looms.

Q: Why did some producers in Europe prefer hand labour to machines?
A: In Victorian Britain labour was cheap and the supply plentiful, so owners saw no need for costly machines. Many goods needed seasonal work, detailed design or small batches that machines could not handle, such as fashion goods and bespoke clothes.
Q: What was the impact of the spinning jenny on workers?
A: It spun many threads at once so fewer workers were needed. Women dependent on spinning for income feared unemployment, so they attacked machines, and the protests continued in the 1790s.
Q: Why did industrialisation start with cotton in Britain?
A: Cotton was a growing consumer market with cheap imports of raw cotton from colonies. New machines made mass production possible and the cotton mills, power looms and later railways formed a base for more growth.
Q: How did colonial rule hurt Indian weavers?
A: Gomasthas controlled prices, supplies and sales. Raw cotton was exported to Britain so weavers faced a shortage, and Manchester cloth was cheaper than hand-made cloth.
Q: Why did Indian textiles decline in the nineteenth century?
A: British machine-made cloth flooded the Indian market, India's exports of cloth to Britain fell as duties rose, and cotton was shipped to England as raw material.
Q: How did the American Civil War affect Indian weavers?
A: The war stopped cotton shipments from the US, so Britain bought Indian raw cotton in large quantities. Prices rose and Indian weavers could not get cotton at a price they could afford.
Q: Who were the early Indian industrialists?
A: They included Dwarkanath Tagore, Dinshaw Petit, Jamsetji Tata, Seth Hukumchand and G. D. Birla, many of whom made fortunes in the China trade and invested in mills and steel.
Q: Why could Indian business grow after the Swadeshi movement?
A: The movement encouraged boycott of British goods and preference for Indian goods, increasing the demand for Indian cloth and other products.
Q: What was a jobber's role in Indian mills?
A: A jobber recruited villagers, helped them get a place and settle in the city, and also demanded money and favours. He gained influence by controlling the workers.
Q: Why were workers in Indian mills mostly from nearby districts?
A: Industrial workers came from villages around the factory. Many remained connected to their villages and went back at harvest time, so the workforce moved with seasons.
Q: How did the First World War help Indian industry?
A: British imports fell as mills made war goods, while Indian mills supplied jute bags, cloth and steel to the army. Demand and profits rose and more workers were employed.
Q: Why did handloom weavers survive in India?
A: They concentrated on finer varieties and fancy designs that mills could not copy easily. Adoption of fly-shuttle looms increased output, so handlooms continued despite mill competition.
Q: How did advertisements help sell goods?
A: Labels, calendars and newspapers made goods familiar and trusted. Pictures of gods and rulers appealed to many people, so even those who could not read recognised the brands.
Q: How did Indian manufacturers use advertisements for nationalism?
A: Their ads carried nationalist messages: if you care for the nation, buy products that Indians make, linking buying to patriotism.
Q: What were the effects of the abolition of the East India Company's monopoly?
A: After 1813 more British traders could trade with India, adding to competition and cheap imports that hurt local weavers.
Q: What is the significance of Manchester labels?
A: Labels carrying the place of manufacture conveyed quality and helped sell British cloth. Indian companies copied them to build trust.
Q: Why are Indian handloom clusters such as Sholapur and Madras known?
A: They survived by making coarse cloth for local use and through the use of the fly shuttle, serving poorer consumers.
Q: Why did industries use jobbers instead of direct hiring?
A: Recruiting workers from villages was hard, and jobbers knew them and could organise them quickly. They controlled workers on behalf of the factory owners.
Q: How did colonial government attitudes shape Indian industry?
A: British officials favoured British goods and exporters, and policies like low tariffs on imports favoured Manchester while limiting Indian industry.
Q: What kinds of Indian industries were mostly set up by the 1900s?
A: Cotton and jute mills dominated, with coal mining, indigo and iron and steel following. Tata Steel started in 1912 in Jamshedpur.

## Moderate
P: What was proto-industrialisation and why did merchants prefer it?
A: It was the phase of industrial production for an international market that was not based on factories. Merchants moved to the countryside, gave advances to peasants and artisans and avoided the restrictions of town guilds, while poor peasants gained extra income.

## High
P: Explain how the East India Company controlled Indian weavers.
A: The Company wanted a monopoly over cotton and silk. It eliminated competition by merchants, appointed paid gomasthas to supervise weavers, collect supplies and examine the quality of cloth, gave advances so weavers could not sell to others, and punished delays. This reduced the weavers' bargaining power, and conflicts rose between weavers and gomasthas.
R: 2 | Monopoly and removal of rivals
R: 2 | Gomasthas to supervise weavers
R: 2 | Advances and ban on selling to others
R: 2 | Conflict and loss of bargaining power
## Advanced
P: Discuss how the First World War and the Swadeshi movement helped Indian industry grow.
A: In the war British mills were busy producing for the army, so Manchester imports to India fell. Indian mills got a huge demand for jute bags, cloth, uniforms and steel and ran multiple shifts, hiring more workers and making large profits. The Swadeshi and boycott movements encouraged Indians to buy Indian goods. After the war Manchester could not recover its old Indian market, so factory production in India expanded and industrialists like Tata and Birla grew in importance.
R: 2 | Fall in imports from Manchester during the war
R: 2 | Army demand for jute, cloth and steel and more shifts
R: 2 | Swadeshi encouraged buying Indian goods
R: 2 | Post-war expansion of Indian industry
