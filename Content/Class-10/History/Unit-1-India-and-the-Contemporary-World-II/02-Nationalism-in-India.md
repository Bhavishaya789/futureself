# Nationalism in India
minutes: 45

@study
Indian nationalism became a mass movement after the First World War, when high prices, taxes, forced recruitment and the influenza epidemic caused hardship. Mahatma Gandhi returned in 1915 and tried satyagraha at Champaran (1917), Kheda (1917) and Ahmedabad (1918). The Rowlatt Act (1919) and the Jallianwala Bagh massacre led to the Non-Cooperation and Khilafat Movement (1920-22), which was called off after Chauri Chaura. The Simon Commission (1927), the Lahore Congress (Purna Swaraj, 1929) and the Salt March (March 1930) began the Civil Disobedience Movement, followed by the Gandhi-Irwin Pact (1931), the Round Table Conferences and the Poona Pact (1932). Peasants, business groups, workers, women and Dalits joined in different ways, and symbols such as Bharat Mata, the tricolour and folklore built a sense of belonging.
## Deep dive: the First World War and Gandhi's early satyagrahas
The war created a new economic and political situation. Defence expenditure was financed by war loans and high taxes: customs duties were raised and income tax introduced. Prices doubled between 1913 and 1918. Villages were forced to supply soldiers. Crop failures of 1918-19 and 1920-21 and an influenza epidemic killed millions, so people were in distress. Gandhi came back from South Africa in January 1915 with the idea of satyagraha, the power of truth and non-violence. He organised satyagraha at Champaran in Bihar (1917) against the oppressive plantation system, at Kheda in Gujarat (1917) for peasants who could not pay revenue after crop failure, and at Ahmedabad (1918) among cotton mill workers.
## Deep dive: Rowlatt Act, Jallianwala Bagh and the Khilafat
The Rowlatt Act of 1919 allowed detention without trial for two years, and Gandhi launched a nationwide satyagraha, with a hartal on 6 April 1919. On 13 April 1919, the Baisakhi day, General Dyer fired on a peaceful crowd in the enclosed Jallianwala Bagh at Amritsar. Hundreds were killed. The Khilafat issue concerned the Ottoman Caliph after the Treaty of Sevres, and the Khilafat Committee led by Muhammad Ali and Shaukat Ali joined with Gandhi to build Hindu-Muslim unity in a broader movement.
## Deep dive: Non-Cooperation Movement 1920-22
At Calcutta (September 1920) and Nagpur (December 1920) the Congress adopted the programme of non-cooperation: surrender of titles, boycott of civil services, army, police, courts, legislative councils, schools and foreign goods. The movement began in January 1921. Students left government schools, lawyers gave up practice, council elections were boycotted (except by the Justice Party in Madras), foreign cloth was burnt and khadi popularised. In the countryside Baba Ramchandra led peasants in Awadh against talukdars and begar; in the Gudem Hills of Andhra, Alluri Sitaram Raju led a militant guerrilla movement; Assam plantation workers left the gardens, defying the Inland Emigration Act of 1859. After a violent clash at Chauri Chaura in February 1922 Gandhi called off the movement.
## Deep dive: Civil Disobedience Movement
The Simon Commission of 1927 had no Indian members and was met with Go Back Simon slogans. In 1929 Lord Irwin announced a vague offer of dominion status, and at Lahore (December 1929) the Congress under Jawaharlal Nehru demanded Purna Swaraj; 26 January 1930 was observed as Independence Day. Gandhi sent an eleven-point list of demands to the Viceroy and began the Salt March from Sabarmati on 12 March 1930 with 78 volunteers, walking about 240 miles to Dandi where he broke the salt law on 6 April. Across India people violated salt laws, boycotted foreign cloth, refused to pay revenue and chaukidari tax and broke forest laws. The Gandhi-Irwin Pact of 5 March 1931 released political prisoners and Gandhi attended the Second Round Table Conference in London, but the movement was relaunched and faded by 1934.

Q: How did the First World War create hardships for Indians?
A: The government raised defence spending by war loans and higher taxes. Customs duties went up and income tax was introduced. Prices doubled between 1913 and 1918, villagers were forced to supply soldiers, and crop failures and an influenza epidemic added to the misery.
Q: What was satyagraha according to Gandhi?
A: Satyagraha was a method of mass agitation that emphasised the power of truth and the need to search for truth. It held that if the cause was true, physical force was not necessary to fight the oppressor, and it relied on non-violence.
Q: Why did the Khilafat Movement matter to nationalism?
A: It was a protest by Muslims against the harsh treatment of the Ottoman Caliph after the war. Gandhi saw a chance to unite Hindus and Muslims under a common national movement against British rule.
Q: Why did some Congress leaders hesitate about non-cooperation in 1920?
A: They feared that boycotting the councils would give a free hand to other groups and that the movement might turn violent. A compromise at the Nagpur session accepted the programme.
Q: How did the Non-Cooperation Movement spread in the cities?
A: Middle-class students left government schools, lawyers gave up practice, councils were boycotted and foreign cloth was burnt. But khadi was costlier than mill cloth, so poor people could not boycott it for long.
Q: Why did the movement slow down in the cities?
A: Khadi cloth was more expensive than mass-produced mill cloth and Indian institutions to replace British ones were few, so students and teachers drifted back to government schools and lawyers to courts.
Q: What was the peasant movement in Awadh?
A: Peasants led by Baba Ramchandra protested against talukdars and landlords who demanded high rents and forced labour, begar. The Oudh Kisan Sabha was formed and Nehru helped. Peasants thought Gandhi would help.
Q: What happened in the Gudem Hills?
A: Tribal peasants resented restrictions on grazing and shifting cultivation. Alluri Sitaram Raju led a guerrilla war, claiming special powers and rejecting non-violence, and was captured and executed in 1924.
Q: How did plantation workers in Assam interpret swaraj?
A: They believed swaraj meant freedom to leave the confines of the plantation and to retain a link with their villages. They left the plantations, defying the Inland Emigration Act of 1859, expecting Gandhi Raj.
Q: Why was the Simon Commission opposed?
A: It was a British-only commission to suggest constitutional changes. Indians were excluded, so all parties welcomed it with black flags and slogans of Go Back Simon.
Q: What was the significance of the Lahore Congress of 1929?
A: The Congress, under Nehru, formally demanded Purna Swaraj, complete independence, and decided to observe 26 January 1930 as Independence Day.
Q: Why did Gandhi choose salt as a symbol?
A: Salt was consumed by the rich and the poor alike and was one of the most essential items of food. The tax on salt and the government monopoly over its production was an oppressive burden.
Q: Describe the Salt March.
A: Gandhi and 78 volunteers walked about 240 miles in 24 days from Sabarmati to Dandi on the Gujarat coast. On 6 April 1930 he picked up salt and violated the salt law, which started civil disobedience.
Q: What forms did civil disobedience take?
A: People broke salt law, boycotted foreign cloth and liquor shops, peasants refused to pay revenue and chaukidari tax, village officials resigned and forest people violated forest laws.
Q: Why did business groups support civil disobedience?
A: They wanted protection against imports of foreign goods and a favourable exchange rate. They formed the Indian Industrial and Commercial Congress in 1920 and FICCI in 1927 and funded the movement.
Q: Why did the industrial workers not participate much?
A: Industrialists were close to the Congress, so workers kept away from the movement. Some workers in Nagpur adopted Gandhian symbols and demands against low wages and poor working conditions.
Q: What role did women play in civil disobedience?
A: Thousands of women, many from high-caste families in urban areas and rich peasant households in rural areas, took part in protest marches, manufactured salt and picketed foreign cloth and liquor shops, and some went to jail.
Q: Why did Dalits not initially join the Congress movement?
A: The Congress had ignored the Dalits for fear of offending the sanatanis, so many Dalit leaders asked for separate electorates and their own political solutions. Ambedkar led this demand.
Q: What was the Poona Pact?
A: After Gandhi's fast unto death against separate electorates, Ambedkar and Gandhi signed the pact in September 1932. It gave Depressed Classes reserved seats in provincial and central councils but voted by the general electorate.
Q: Why did the Muslim League and Hindu Mahasabha grow after 1922?
A: After the Khilafat Movement collapsed, relations between Hindus and Muslims soured. Communal riots broke out and the two organisations expanded.
Q: How did images help build nationalism?
A: The image of Bharat Mata, first painted by Abanindranath Tagore as an ascetic figure, gave the nation a visible form. Later versions showed her as a mother figure which people could worship.
Q: How did folklore and history strengthen nationalism?
A: Nationalists collected folk tales, ballads and songs to rediscover an authentic culture, and Indians wrote histories showing past glories to take pride and oppose colonial claims.
Q: What were the limits of the Civil Disobedience Movement?
A: It lacked wide Dalit and Muslim participation. Many Muslims were alienated by the Congress's close links with Hindu religious symbols, and the movement faded by 1934.

## Moderate
P: Why did Gandhi launch a nationwide satyagraha against the Rowlatt Act in 1919?
A: The Rowlatt Act gave the government power to repress political activities and allowed detention of political prisoners without trial for two years. Gandhi saw it as an unjust law passed against the unanimous opposition of Indian members, so he organised a hartal on 6 April 1919 and a non-violent civil disobedience campaign against it.

## High
P: Explain why the Non-Cooperation Movement began and why Gandhi called it off in 1922.
A: It began because of the Jallianwala Bagh massacre, the Rowlatt Act and the Khilafat issue; Gandhi felt Indians cooperated with British rule and could end it by withdrawing cooperation: boycotting schools, courts, councils and foreign goods. In February 1922 a crowd at Chauri Chaura set a police station on fire, killing policemen. Gandhi feared violence and called off the movement because satyagraha had to be non-violent and masses were not yet disciplined.

## Advanced
P: Discuss the participation of different social groups in the Civil Disobedience Movement and how the Poona Pact tried to settle the Dalit question.
A: Rich peasants such as Patidars and Jats, hit by falling prices, supported it and wanted revenue reduced. Business groups, including FICCI leaders like Birla and Purshottamdas Thakurdas, backed it to end colonial restrictions on trade. Workers in Nagpur joined in some places. Women marched, picketed and went to jail. Many Dalits stayed away until Ambedkar organised the Depressed Classes Association in 1930 and demanded separate electorates. Gandhi began a fast unto death against this and under the Poona Pact of September 1932 the Depressed Classes got reserved seats in provincial and central councils, to be voted by the general electorate.
