# Print Culture and the Modern World
minutes: 40
yt: class 10 history print culture and the modern world
@study
Printing began in China, Japan and Korea with hand-printing from woodblocks. Marco Polo brought the technique to Europe, and in the 1430s Johann Gutenberg built the printing press in Strasbourg, producing the Bible. Print spread ideas: Martin Luther's Ninety-Five Theses (1517) fuelled the Reformation, the Church created the Index of Prohibited Books, and reading culture helped the Enlightenment and the French Revolution. New presses, cheap books, novels and magazines created new readers including women and workers. In India print came with the Portuguese in Goa, and newspapers, social reform, religious debate and women's writing grew in the nineteenth century, while the colonial government answered with the Vernacular Press Act of 1878.
## Deep dive: the first printed books
Printing technology developed in China, Japan and Korea where books were printed by hand with woodblocks. China had a large number of educated officials and the state was the major producer of printed material. Buddhist missionaries brought printing to Japan around AD 768-770, and the oldest Japanese book, the Buddhist Diamond Sutra, was printed in AD 868. In Japan Kitagawa Utamaro produced ukiyo pictures of the floating world. Marco Polo brought woodblock printing from China to Italy in 1295, and soon books were printed in Europe on woodblocks, mainly for the rich and for Church use.
## Deep dive: Gutenberg and the print revolution
Johann Gutenberg, the son of a merchant, grew up on a large agricultural estate and saw wine and olive presses. He adapted that technology to design a printing press with metal moulds for the alphabet in the 1430s at Strasbourg. The first book he printed was the Bible, and about 180 copies were printed in three years. Presses spread across Europe and between 1450 and 1550 printing presses were set up in most countries. The new technology changed how people read and thought: they could read cheap books, and there was an explosion of reading. Martin Luther wrote Ninety-Five Theses in 1517 criticising the practices of the Roman Catholic Church, and his translation of the New Testament sold 5,000 copies in a few weeks, which helped begin the Protestant Reformation. In 1558 the Church began to maintain an Index of Prohibited Books, and Menocchio, an Italian miller, was executed for his reading of the Bible and other books.
## Deep dive: the print and the modern world
Print popularised ideas of the Enlightenment through writers such as Voltaire and Rousseau, created a public debate and prepared the ground for the French Revolution. Louis-Sebastien Mercier said the printing press was the powerful engine of progress and public opinion would sweep despotism away. In the nineteenth century the steam-powered cylindrical press of Richard M. Hoe printed 8,000 sheets per hour, the offset press and electrically operated presses followed, and publishers produced penny magazines, novels, almanacs and cheap series. Women became readers and writers, including Jane Austen, the Bronte sisters and George Eliot; children's books such as the Grimm brothers' fairy tales were published.
## Deep dive: print in India
Printing came to India with the Portuguese missionaries in Goa in the mid-sixteenth century. Catholic priests printed the first Tamil book in 1579 at Cochin and the first Malayalam book in 1713, and Dutch Protestant missionaries printed Tamil texts. James Augustus Hicky began the Bengal Gazette in 1780, and Gangadhar Bhattacharya started the first Indian weekly in 1816. Raja Rammohan Roy's Sambad Kaumudi appeared in 1821 and the Bombay Samachar in 1822. Printing fed religious debate through tracts and newspapers, helped social reform through writers like Jyotiba Phule (Gulamgiri, 1871), allowed women such as Rashsundari Debi (Amar Jiban, 1876) and Tarabai Shinde (Stripurushtulna, 1882) to publish, and raised nationalism. The Vernacular Press Act of 1878 gave the government the right to censor reports and editorials.

A: Gutenberg knew the olive and wine presses. He adapted the screw-press, and made metal moulds of the alphabet so that pages could be set and printed many times.
Q: Why did print help the Reformation?
A: Luther's ideas were printed and spread widely in a short time. The translated New Testament was sold in thousands of copies, so ordinary people could read the Bible and question the Church.
Q: What was the Church's reaction to the spread of print?
A: It feared that heretical ideas would spread so it controlled publishers and booksellers and in 1558 maintained an Index of Prohibited Books.
Q: Why were new readers created by print?
A: Books became cheaper and could be read aloud to the illiterate. Libraries and reading groups grew and popular ballads and stories were printed for farmers and artisans.
Q: How did print help the Enlightenment?
A: Works of thinkers such as Voltaire and Rousseau were printed and discussed widely. They urged people to question custom and rely on reason, forming a public opinion that challenged absolute monarchies.
Q: How did print prepare for the French Revolution?
A: Books and cartoons criticised the monarchy and Church, and ideas of liberty and reason spread. People read and debated the new ideas in public, which made revolution thinkable.
Q: What changes in printing happened in the nineteenth century?
A: Hoe's steam-powered cylindrical press printed 8,000 sheets an hour, offset printing and electric presses followed, and automatic paper reels increased speed and cut the cost of newspapers and books.
Q: How did women become part of the reading public?
A: Novels, penny magazines and serials attracted women. Writers like Jane Austen and the Bronte sisters wrote about women's lives and women became both readers and authors.

A: It was a commercial weekly started by James Augustus Hicky in 1780, proud of being independent of colonial influence, and it attacked the Company officials.
Q: How did the print revive religious debate in India?
A: Newspapers and tracts carried discussions among Hindu and Muslim reformers and Christian missionaries about customs and beliefs, and religious texts such as the Ramcharitmanas reached many homes in print.
Q: How did the print help social reform?
A: Reformers like Rammohan Roy and Phule wrote against sati, child marriage and caste. Newspapers spread their ideas and let people discuss reform.
Q: Why were women's writings important in India?
A: They showed women's views and experiences, such as Rashsundari Debi's Amar Jiban, and encouraged women to read and write despite social restrictions.
Q: How did the print help nationalism in India?
A: Newspapers criticised colonial rule and reported the activities of nationalists. Printed songs and pamphlets spread a common identity.
Q: Why did the British pass the Vernacular Press Act in 1878?
A: The government worried about the growing criticism in Indian language newspapers. The Act, modelled on Irish press laws, let it censor reports and seize press equipment.
Q: How did Indians respond to the Vernacular Press Act?
A: Nationalist papers protested and continued to publish. The Act strengthened the demand for freedom of the press and nationalism.

A: It shows the Enlightenment faith that print could spread reason, build public opinion and end despotism.
Q: How did the print reduce the cost of books?
A: Mechanised presses printed many copies faster and cheaply, and cheaper paper and distribution made books affordable to the middle class and workers.

## Moderate
P: How did Gutenberg's printing press change the way books were produced?
A: Gutenberg adapted wine and olive presses and used metal moulds for the alphabet to print a page quickly. About 180 copies of the Bible were printed in three years, far faster than hand copying, so books became cheaper and spread among common people.

## High
P: Explain how print helped Martin Luther and the Reformation, and how the Catholic Church reacted.
A: Luther wrote the Ninety-Five Theses in 1517 criticising Church practices. Printed copies spread widely, and his New Testament translation sold thousands of copies in weeks, so reformers reached large audiences. The Church feared the new ideas, began to censor publishers and booksellers, and from 1558 kept an Index of Prohibited Books. The Italian miller Menocchio was executed for his reading.

## Advanced
P: Discuss the role of print in nineteenth-century India in religion, social reform and nationalism, and how the government responded.
A: Printed tracts and newspapers carried religious debate among Hindu and Muslim reformers and Christian missionaries. Social reformers like Rammohan Roy and Jyotiba Phule used print for reform and to attack caste; women like Rashsundari Debi and Tarabai Shinde wrote and published. Newspapers, including Bengal Gazette and Kesari, criticised colonial rule and spread nationalism. The colonial government feared this and passed the Vernacular Press Act of 1878 which allowed it to censor reports and editorials and later used sedition laws.
