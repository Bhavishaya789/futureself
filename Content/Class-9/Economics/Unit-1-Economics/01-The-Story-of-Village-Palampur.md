# The story of village Palampur
minutes: 35
yt: class 9 economics story of village palampur
@study
Palampur is a model village in western Uttar Pradesh used to explain production. Production needs four factors: land, labour, physical capital and human capital. Land is fixed, so farmers use multiple cropping and modern methods (Green Revolution: HYV seeds, chemical fertilisers, irrigation) to raise output. Farming is the main activity; non-farm activities are dairy, small manufacturing, shopkeeping and transport. Wages of farm labourers are low and many families own little or no land.
## Deep dive: the village and its farming
Palampur is well connected by road to the nearby village Raiganj and the small town Shahpur, has electricity, a primary and a high school, a primary health centre and a private dispensary. Of about 450 families, 150 have no land for cultivation (mostly dalits); 240 families cultivate small plots of less than 2 hectares; 60 medium and large farmers cultivate more than 2 hectares and some own 10 hectares or more. Land area is fixed, so the same land must produce more. Farmers grow two or more crops a year (multiple cropping): jowar and bajra in the rainy season, potato in between and wheat in winter, with sugarcane harvested for jaggery.
## Deep dive: the Green Revolution and its effects
Traditional farming used cow-dung manure, local seeds and rain. In the late 1960s the Green Revolution introduced high yielding varieties (HYV) of seeds, chemical fertilisers, pesticides and assured irrigation (tubewells, electricity). Wheat yield in Palampur rose from about 1300 kg per hectare to 3200 kg per hectare. Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh gained the most. Drawbacks: chemical fertilisers reduce soil fertility, over-use of groundwater lowers the water table, and the cost of cultivation rises, hurting small farmers. Sustainable farming protects land and water.
## Deep dive: factors of production
Land includes soil, water, forests and minerals; labour is the physical and mental effort of workers; physical capital includes fixed capital (tools, machines, buildings) and working capital (raw materials and cash). Human capital is the knowledge and enterprise needed to combine land, labour and capital to produce output. Capital is needed in large amounts and small farmers often borrow from moneylenders at high interest, while large farmers use their own savings.
## Deep dive: non-farm activities and farm labour
Only about a fourth of the workers in a village are in non-farm work. Examples in Palampur: dairy (buffaloes, selling milk in Raiganj), small-scale manufacturing using simple methods and family labour (such as jaggery making), shopkeeping, and transport (rickshaws, tongas, jeeps, tractors, bullock carts). Farm labourers are landless and earn wages in cash or kind; they are poor because there is more labour than work and wages are often below the minimum set by the government. Better irrigation, roads, markets and credit can create more non-farm jobs.
