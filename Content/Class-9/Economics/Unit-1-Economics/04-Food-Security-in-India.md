# Food security in India
minutes: 35
yt: class 9 economics food security in india
@study
Food security means availability, accessibility and affordability of food to all people at all times. India achieved self-sufficiency in foodgrains after the Green Revolution. The government keeps a buffer stock through the Food Corporation of India (FCI) and distributes grain through the Public Distribution System (PDS) with ration shops at subsidised prices. Minimum support price (MSP) is announced for farmers. The National Food Security Act 2013 gives legal rights to subsidised grain.
## Deep dive: what is food security
Food security means that food is available (produced within the country, imported and in government stocks), accessible (within reach of every person) and affordable (people have money to buy enough safe and nutritious food). It also means a balanced diet. Food insecurity can be chronic (a continuing lack of food because of low income) or seasonal (related to harvests and the farming cycle). The most food insecure are the landless, small farmers, artisans, casual labourers, the destitute, and women and children; regions such as Bihar, Jharkhand, Odisha and Uttar Pradesh are worst affected. Famines such as the Bengal famine of 1943, which killed about 30 lakh people, showed the danger.
## Deep dive: the Green Revolution and self-sufficiency
After independence, India aimed at self-sufficiency in foodgrains. The Green Revolution of the late 1960s (HYV seeds of wheat and rice, fertilisers, irrigation) made India self-sufficient, mostly in Punjab, Haryana and Uttar Pradesh. Foodgrain production rose from about 50 million tonnes in 1950-51 to over 250 million tonnes. Yet some people remain hungry because of poor purchasing power. The White Revolution of Amul and the cooperative milk movement raised milk supply.
## Deep dive: buffer stock, MSP and PDS
The Food Corporation of India (FCI, set up in 1965) buys wheat and rice from farmers at a Minimum Support Price (MSP) announced before the sowing season. The grain forms the buffer stock, which is kept to be used in shortage and to stabilise prices. It is distributed through the Public Distribution System (PDS) in ration shops (fair price shops) at a price lower than the market price, the issue price. Card types: Antyodaya cards for the poorest, BPL (below poverty line) and APL (above poverty line). Rationing began during the 1940s Bengal famine. The Revamped PDS (1992) and Targeted PDS (1997) improved delivery; the Antyodaya Anna Yojana (2000) provides cheap grain to the poorest.
## Deep dive: current schemes and problems
National Food Security Act 2013 gives subsidised food grain to about two-thirds of the population: 5 kg per person per month, with the poorest AAY households getting 35 kg per family. Other schemes: Mid-day Meal, ICDS, Food for Work and the Annapurna Scheme for senior citizens. Problems of PDS: ration shop dealers divert grain to the open market, poor quality of grain, irregular opening, and growing stocks with high storage costs. Cooperatives such as Amul and Mother Dairy and NGOs help; grain banks and Academy of Development Science work in Maharashtra. Reforms include technology for tracking and direct benefit transfer.
