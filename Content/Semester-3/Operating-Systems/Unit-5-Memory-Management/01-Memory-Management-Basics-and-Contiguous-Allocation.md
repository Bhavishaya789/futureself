Programs must be in main memory to run. The OS decides where each process goes and protects it from others.

## Address binding
- **Logical (virtual) address:** generated by the CPU, seen by the program.
- **Physical address:** the real location in memory.
- Binding can happen at **compile time** (absolute code), **load time** (relocatable code), or **execution time** (needs hardware support; used by modern OSs).
The **Memory Management Unit (MMU)** translates logical to physical addresses. In the simplest scheme a **relocation (base) register** is added to every logical address, and a **limit register** checks that the address is within the process's size. Protection: if `logical ≥ limit`, trap to the OS.

## Swapping
A process can be temporarily **swapped out** to a backing store (disk) and later brought back, so total memory used can exceed physical memory. Swapping is slow, so systems limit it.

## Contiguous allocation
Each process occupies **one contiguous block**.
- **Fixed (static) partitions:** memory is divided into fixed-size partitions. Simple, but suffers **internal fragmentation** (unused space inside an allocated partition).
- **Variable (dynamic) partitions:** a partition is made exactly as large as the process. Leads to **external fragmentation**: enough total free space, but scattered in pieces too small to use.

### Placement strategies for a hole
- **First fit:** first hole big enough. Fast.
- **Best fit:** smallest hole that is big enough. Leaves tiny leftovers; must search the whole list.
- **Worst fit:** largest hole. Leaves large leftovers; usually worst in practice.
Example: holes of 100, 500, 200, 300, 600 KB and requests 212, 417, 112, 426 KB in order.
| Strategy | 212 | 417 | 112 | 426 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First fit | 500 hole | 600 hole | 288 left of 500 | not allocated |
| Best fit | 300 hole | 500 hole | 200 hole | 600 hole |
| Worst fit | 600 hole | 500 hole | 388 left of 600 | not allocated |
Best fit uses memory most efficiently here: all four processes are placed.

## Fragmentation
- **Internal:** wasted space inside an allocated block.
- **External:** wasted space between blocks.
**Compaction** shifts processes together to make one large hole (needs execution-time binding and is costly). **Paging** removes external fragmentation altogether.

## The 50-percent rule
With first fit, for every N allocated blocks about N/2 blocks are lost to fragmentation, so one third of memory may be unusable.

> Fixed partitions waste inside (internal fragmentation); variable partitions waste between (external fragmentation).
@qa
Q: What is the difference between a logical and a physical address?
A: A logical address is generated by the CPU and used by the program. A physical address is the actual location in main memory. The MMU maps logical to physical.
Q: What is the role of the limit register?
A: It holds the size of the process's address space, and any logical address beyond it causes a trap, which protects other processes.
Q: Differentiate internal and external fragmentation.
A: Internal fragmentation is unused space inside an allocated partition. External fragmentation is free memory split into small non-contiguous holes that cannot satisfy a request.
Q: Which placement strategy is usually best?
A: First fit and best fit are generally better than worst fit; first fit is faster, best fit wastes the least in many cases.
Q: What is compaction?
A: Moving processes in memory to combine all free holes into one large block. It needs dynamic (execution-time) relocation and takes time.

## Moderate
P: Memory partitions of 100K, 500K, 200K, 300K, 600K (in order). Place processes of 212K, 417K, 112K and 426K using first fit and best fit. Which processes are placed in each case?
A: First fit: 212 goes to 500 (leaves 288), 417 goes to 600 (leaves 183), 112 goes to 288 (the remainder of the 500K partition), 426 does not fit anywhere and must wait.
Best fit: 212 goes to 300, 417 goes to 500, 112 goes to 200, 426 goes to 600. All four are placed.

## High
P: Explain internal and external fragmentation. How does compaction help and what is its cost?
A: Internal fragmentation is wasted space inside an allocated block (a process gets a 4K block but needs 3K). External fragmentation is free memory split into many small holes that are not contiguous, so a large request fails even though the total free space is enough.
Compaction moves processes together so free holes merge into one large block. It is costly because it needs CPU time to copy memory and requires dynamic relocation.
