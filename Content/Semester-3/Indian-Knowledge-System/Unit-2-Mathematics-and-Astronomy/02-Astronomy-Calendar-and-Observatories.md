## Jyotisha and Siddhantas
Early Indian astronomy (*Vedanga Jyotisha*) was tied to calendars and rituals. The classical **Siddhanta** texts (Surya Siddhanta, Aryabhatiya, Brahmasphutasiddhanta, Siddhanta Shiromani) calculate planetary positions, eclipses and time.

## Key ideas
- **Aryabhata:** Earth rotates on its axis (apparent motion of stars due to Earth's rotation); eclipses are due to shadows of Earth and Moon; length of the sidereal year about 365.2587 days (365 days 6 h 12 min 30 s).
- Use of sine (jya) in astronomical computation; words *sine* and *cosine* trace back through Arabic from the Sanskrit jya.
- Lunar and solar motion models, epicycles, and tables (karanas) for computation.

## The Panchang
The traditional almanac with five elements: **tithi** (lunar day), **vara** (weekday), **nakshatra** (lunar mansion; 27), **yoga**, **karana** (half tithi). India uses many regional calendars: lunisolar (Vikrama Samvat, Shaka Samvat) and solar (Tamil, Bengali, Malayalam). The Indian national calendar based on the Shaka era was adopted in 1957. Festivals follow lunisolar dates, with an extra month (adhika masa) inserted about every three years to align with the solar year.

## Units
Time units from the tiny truti to the large yuga cycles; the day was divided into 60 ghatis.

## Observatories
**Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh II** (1688–1743) built five observatories (Jantar Mantar) in Delhi, Jaipur, Ujjain, Varanasi and Mathura in the early 18th century. The Jaipur one, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, includes the **Samrat Yantra**, a giant sundial that tells time to about two seconds accuracy, and instruments for finding the position of stars and the zodiac.

## Modern achievements
ISRO's Chandrayaan, Mangalyaan (Mars Orbiter Mission, 2013–14, first Asian mission to reach Mars orbit and the first to do so on the first attempt), Aditya-L1 (Sun), and the work of Indian astrophysicists such as S. Chandrasekhar (Chandrasekhar limit, Nobel 1983) and Meghnad Saha.

## Sundial idea
A gnomon's shadow length changes with the Sun's altitude. At noon on an equinox at latitude φ, shadow length/gnomon height = tan φ. For Dehradun (about 30.3° N): tan 30.3° ≈ 0.58.

> Indian astronomy blended careful observation, mathematics and calendar-making.

## High
P: Why is the Hindu calendar called lunisolar? A lunar month is 29.53 days and a solar year is 365.24 days. Find the length of 12 lunar months, the yearly gap and how often an extra month is needed.
A: Months follow the Moon (lunar), while the year is kept in step with the seasons using the Sun, so it is lunisolar. 12 x 29.53 = 354.4 days. Gap = 365.24 - 354.37 = about 10.9 days per year. An extra month (Adhika Masa) is needed after 29.53 / 10.9 = about 2.7 years, so roughly 7 times in 19 years.

## Advanced
P: Describe the Jantar Mantar observatories: who built them, what is the Samrat Yantra and how does a sundial of that kind tell time? Give two astronomical ideas of Aryabhata.
A: Jantar Mantar observatories were built in the early 1700s by Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh II at Delhi, Jaipur, Ujjain, Varanasi and Mathura. The Samrat Yantra is a huge stone sundial: a triangular gnomon points to the celestial pole, its shadow falls on a calibrated quadrant scale and gives local time to a precision of seconds.
Aryabhata proposed that the Earth rotates on its axis, causing the apparent motion of stars, and explained eclipses as shadows of the Earth and the Moon.
